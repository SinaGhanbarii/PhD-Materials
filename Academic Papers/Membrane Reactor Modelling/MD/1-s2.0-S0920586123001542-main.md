### **1. Introduction** 

Synthesis gas (syngas), a mixture of carbon oxides (CO and CO2) and H2, is the platform for important chemicals and fuels such as methanol, Fischer–Tropsch (FT) liquids, and dimethyl ether (DME). The current route to syngas involves steam reforming, autothermal reforming, (ATR) or partial oxidation (POX) of natural gas [1]. While these technologies are suited for large scale productions ( _e.g., >_ ~10<sup>5</sup> barrels/day of FT liquids) to reduce the unit cost of making syngas [2], they inherently escalate the volume of CO2 emitted to the atmosphere along with other undesirable species. Increased awareness on the negative impacts of the anthropogenic emissions of CO2 promotes incentives for the technologies enabling its capture and conversion. In this regard, CO2 is considered as a carbon source that can react with H2 to several value–added products such as methanol, DME, FT liquids ( _e.g.,_ sustainable aviation fuel, gasoline, diesel), with the type and the purity of the product depending primarily on the catalyst type and reaction conditions. Availability of the required H2 _via_ solar or wind–powered water 

electrolysis makes the CO2 hydrogenation route even more promising in terms of reduced carbon footprint [3–5]. Conditions and catalyst configurations of CO2 hydrogenation can also be tuned to produce syngas _via_ the reverse water–gas shift reaction (RWGS): CO2 + H2⇌CO + H2O _,_ Δ _H_<sup>_o_</sup> = 42 _._ 1 kJ mol<sup>–1</sup> (1) 

The reaction is endothermic and thermodynamically favored to produce CO as a major product at temperatures in excess of 973 K depending on the relative amounts of molar inlet CO2 and H2 [6,7]. However, RWGS reaction competes with the unwanted exothermic Sabatier reaction that is thermodynamically promoted at lower temperatures: CO2 + 4H2⇌CH4 + 2H2O _,_ Δ _H_<sup>_o_</sup> = –165 kJ mol<sup>–1</sup> (2) 

Catalyst type plays a crucial role in shaping–up the selectivity towards the reactions. Cu, Pt and Pd–based catalysts generally favor RWGS, whereas Ni, Rh and Ru catalysts are more selective to Reaction (2) [6–8]. In the so–called chemical looping (CL) aided RWGS, oxygen transfer material is used in a way that it is first reduced to transform H2 into H2O and then oxidized again by taking oxygen from CO2 to produce CO [9–11]. Since the reduction and oxidation of the oxygen transfer material occur separately, the possibility of methane formation _via_ Reaction (2) is eliminated, and the gas separation is simplified significantly. The conventional catalytic RWGS temperatures of ~973 K can be reduced to ~723-823 K by chemical looping [9-11]. These temperatures can be reduced further by _in_ – _situ_ removal of water _via_ using a steam–selective adsorbent or a membrane, which assists in widening the thermodynamic window and increases the operability of the catalysts at temperatures as low as ~523 K [12–15]. When this is combined with an active catalyst capable of driving CO2 hydrogenation selectively to CO formation at low temperatures, the RWGS route can be considered as a promising alternative for making syngas. Owing to its cyclic nature and the periodic demand on heat input for adsorbent regeneration, the sorption–enhanced RWGS is conceptually less practical than the membrane–integrated configuration capable of being operated continuously operation without extra energy need to drive the membrane separation. 

The use of steam–selective membranes in catalytic reactions that involve steam as an unwanted product is receiving interest in recent years. Experimental and modeling studies on DME synthesis _via_ hydrogenation of CO2 [16] or CO + CO2 mixtures [17–21] commonly point out substantial improvement in DME production upon decoration of the catalytic reactor with hydrophilic membranes such as Linde Type A (LTA) or hydroxy sodalite (H–SOD). Significant improvements in membrane assisted CO2–to–methanol and FT processes are clearly presented in the reviews of van Kampen et al. [12] and Li et al. [13]. Unlike these processes, studies on membrane–assisted RWGS are limited. Dzuryk and Rezaei [22] modeled the RWGS reaction in a packed–bed membrane reactor comprising a CuO/ZnO/Al2O3 catalyst and a 

steam–selective membrane. The authors studied the effects of molar inlet H2/CO2 ratio, H2O/H2 permselectivity, gas hourly space velocity, and relative values of the pressures and flow rates of the sweep (N2) and reactive mixture in the co– and counter–current modes. They reported that CO2 conversion could be increased from ~15% to ~60% upon membrane integration at H2:CO2 = 3 and at a temperature of 523 K, which was significantly below that of the ( _i.e., >_ ~973 K) conventional catalytic RWGS. These findings were coherent with the thermodynamic predictions that showed the possibility of exceeding the pertinent theoretical barrier upon membrane incorporation [23]. The benefits of _in_ – _situ_ membrane separation of H2O in the context of methanol synthesis _via_ RWGS were shown by Samimi et al. [24]. Comparison of the membraneless and membrane–integrated scenarios revealed ~4% improvement in methanol production rate in the latter. 

The limited number of studies on membrane integrated RGWS operation commonly consider two concentric tubes with the particulate catalyst being packed into the inner tube whose wall functions as a membrane. In this design, surface area of the membrane per reactor volume can be increased by reducing the diameter of the inner tube to fit more of them into the outer tube and/or by increasing the tube length. However, these actions elevate frictional pressure loss in the inner tube [25]. Further volumetric intensification without increasing pressure drop is possible by structuring the flow paths to micro/milli–scales [26]. Decoration of the layers of membrane between the neighboring permeate and catalyst washcoated reaction channels can increase the surface area per volume of the resulting unit by a factor of ~10<sup>2</sup> [27–29]. Moreover, assuming identical feeding to the permeate and reaction channels, and minimization of the variations in the channel dimensions through high precision micro–machining and reproducible catalyst coating techniques, the unit can be scaled up simply by numbering–up the channels according to the desired capacity [30,31]. Miniaturized dimensions can also promote heat exchange between the neighboring channels allowing the possibility of integrating heating or cooling function with the reaction and/or membrane separation [18,19, 26,32]. These features are demonstrated in the computational modeling of a membrane microchannel reactor for CO2 hydrogenation to DME [16]. In addition to the capability of delivering isothermal conditions, the reactor offered CO2 conversion and DME yield up to ~73% and ~35%, respectively, both of which were _ca._ three times higher than those obtained in the membraneless counterpart. In the light of these studies reporting the advantages of volumetric and functional intensification on reactor performance, RWGS reaction is comprehensively modeled in a microstructured catalytic membrane reactor. Pure H2, one of the readily available reactants, is tested as the sweep gas to enable simultaneous efflux of H2O from and influx of H2 to the reaction zone for boosting kinetic and thermodynamic promotion of the RGWS. After validating it with the literature–based experimental data, the reactor model is used to understand the effects of inlet velocities and partitioning of the reactive mixture and sweep streams, reactor pressure and molar inlet H2:CO2 ratio on reactor performance. The opposing relation between, per cent CO2 conversion and the amount of CO2 consumed, the performance metrics used herein, are demonstrated and the optimum set of operating conditions are determined by considering syngas composition as a constraint. Additionally, volume and catalyst–based productivities of the proposed membrane microchannel reactor are compared with those of a packed–bed membrane reactor under equivalent conditions. To our best knowledge, the findings reported herein provide new and useful insight into efficient syngas production with flexible composition at mild operating conditions.

Isothermal, steady state operation of the unit cell (Fig. 1b), the repeating core geometry of the multichannel reactor shown in Fig. 1a, is modeled by the simultaneous solution of the mass and momentum conservation equations in the fluid phases of the reaction and permeate channels, reactive transport in the porous catalyst layer and interchannel transfer of H2O and H2 across the SOD membrane layer. The model considers _x_ and _y_ coordinates as the independent variables, and the changes in the _z_ coordinate, the third spatial dimension, are neglected. This simplification is based on the negligible differences between 2D and 3D models set up for a similar reaction domain and conditions [40], and offers notable reductions in the computational cost. The model equations, correlations and mixing laws needed for the estimation of the physical properties of the fluids ( _e.g._ , density, viscosity, gas phase and effective diffusivities), which are assumed to follow Newtonian behavior, and the boundary conditions are presented in SI. The set of partial differential equations are solved numerically by the finite volume method (ANSYS, v. 19.2). For this purpose, zones of the unit cell (Fig. 1b) are meshed with a total of 5.76 × 10<sup>5</sup> square cells, each having an edge length of 1.25 × 10<sup>–5</sup> m. This configuration refers to the finest meshing possible with the Gambit platform, so no additional cases are studied for the mesh independency. Reaction kinetics (Eq. (3)), binary diffusivity correlations (SI, Table S3) are integrated to the numerical solution in the form of user defined functions (UDF), each of which is called and evaluated at every nodal point. 

Transport of H2O and H2 across the SOD membrane is modeled by creating a wall with zero thickness between the channels. In order to compute membrane mass flux, mesh cells neighboring the zero–thickness wall are defined as sink and source zones, which are assigned with source terms involving mass inflow and outflow of H2O and H2, respectively. The membrane UDF is integrated to the reactor model equations presented in SI (Table S2). Data needed for executing the membrane model is adapted from the literature [34]. At 473 K, the SOD membrane is reported to have high steam perm–selectivity to CO and CO2, so permeation of carbon oxides is not considered. Cross–membrane transport of H2 is justified by the relatively lower H2O/H2 selectivity of 4.6. Steam permeability of the SOD membrane is reported as 6.5 × 10<sup>–8</sup> mol m<sup>–2</sup> s<sup>–1</sup> Pa<sup>–1</sup> [34]. Considering that this experimental data is reported at 473 K for the membrane separation of the binary mixtures, permeability of H2O is taken as 3 × 10<sup>–8</sup> mol m<sup>–2</sup> s<sup>–1</sup> Pa<sup>–1</sup> to account for (a) the multicomponent nature of the gas mixture that can cause competitive adsorption to the membrane surface and hinder permeability [41,42], and (b) the lack of experimental data at 523 K. It was shown that the contributions of promoted permeation of H2O and H2 on the performance of an SOD membrane–integrated microreactor become negligible when the permeability exceeds 3 × 10<sup>–8</sup> mol m<sup>–2</sup> s<sup>–1</sup> Pa<sup>–1</sup> [16]. This finding is used as a justification for fixing H2O permeability to 3 × 10<sup>–8</sup> mol m<sup>–2</sup> s<sup>–1</sup> Pa<sup>–1</sup> in the current study. In order to quantify the benefits of integrated separation, a set of benchmark simulations are run in the absence of membrane. For this purpose, an impermeable wall is imposed simply by nullifying the source terms that account for interchannel mass transfer in the membrane model. Outcomes of the membrane–integrated simulations are also benchmarked against equilibrium CO2 conversion computed as described in SI (Section A).

### **3. Results and discussion** 

_3.1. Comparison of the reactor model simulations with literature_ – _based experimental data_ 

Prior to its use in understanding the characteristics of the membrane–integrated RWGS reaction, outcomes of the reactor model are compared with the literature–based experimental data reported for the CuO/ZnO/Al2O3 catalyst [37]. Integrated separation function is disabled in the model as the experimental study was run in a membraneless packed–bed reactor. In this regard, the reactor model quantifies the operation of the catalyst washcoated microchannel ( _i.e._ , the reaction channel depicted in Fig. 1b), and comprises conservation equations formulated in two spatial dimensions at steady state, correlations and mixing rules for the estimation of fluid properties (SI, Section B) and the kinetic model (Eq. (3)). The membraneless reactor model is run at 523 K, 1 bar, H2:CO2 = 6 and within the _W/F_ CO2 _,_ in range of ~2–44 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> due to the availability of the experimental CO2 conversions at these conditions. Absence of the transport resistances in both reactors allows direct comparison of the data produced on a particulate bed of catalyst with the data currently computed on the washcoated form of the same catalyst. The results shows that experimental and simulated CO2 conversions are satisfactorily close to each other at _W/F_ CO2 _,_ in _>_ ~28 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> ; at _W/F_ CO2 _,_ in= 40 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> , the residence time used in the current study, reactor model successfully captures the experimental CO2 conversion (Fig. 2). On the other hand, the reactor model underpredicts the experimental data at _W/F_ CO2 _,_ in _<_ ~28 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> . This deviation observed at _p_ = 1 bar is believed to be due to the pressure correction factor ( _C_ FP, defined in SI (Eq. (S2)) in the reaction rate expression (Eq. (3)). At the default pressure of 15 bar (Table 1), the mathematical model is expected to give higher CO2 conversion closer to the experimental data as the reaction rate increases due to the change in _C_ FP from 1.0 (at 1 bar) to 3.3 (at 15 bar)). The pronounced impact of _C_ FP at 15 bar is expected to be observed at low residence times where CO2 conversion is dictated mostly by the reaction kinetics. At residence times _>_ ~28 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> , however, the slightly reduced rate of increase in CO2 conversion indicates the impact of thermodynamic equilibrium which limits the contribution of _C_ FP. Therefore, the overlap between the experimental and predicted CO2 conversions at _W/F_ CO2 _,_ in= 40 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> shown for 1 bar (Fig. 2) is expected to remain almost unaffected at 15 bar. Since the sweep gas is pure H2, the positive partial pressure gradient allows transmembrane efflux of H2O from the reaction channel, leading to further promotion of the kinetic and thermodynamic effects to boost CO2 conversion. These 

phenomena become more pronounced at higher amounts of sweep gas (H2) as the permeate side H2O becomes less concentrated, which maintains the driving force for H2O transport across the membrane layer. ### _3.4. Comparison of the packed_ – _bed and microchannel reactor configurations_ 

The results discussed in the previous sections highlight the potentials of the membrane–integrated microreactor in overcoming thermodynamic limitations and running RWGS at 523 K which is below the temperatures reported for the conventional catalytic route [6–8]. In an attempt for understanding the effect of the reactor architecture on CO2 utilization _via_ RWGS, the current configuration (Fig. 1) is benchmarked with a packed–bed membrane reactor (PBMR) under equivalent conditions. Comparison is based on the residence time ( _W/F_ CO2 _,_ in) of 40 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> and carried out at 523 K, 5 bar and H2:CO2 = 3. Details regarding sizing and modeling of the microchannel and PBMR are provided in Section 2 and SI (Section D), respectively. 

The results, presented in Fig. 8, show that the microchannel membrane reactor outperforms the PBMR; the former offers almost ~2.5 times higher molar CO2 consumption per mass of catalyst and reactor volume, and delivers ~33% higher CO2 conversion. Considering that both reactors are modeled to run without being masked by transport limitations, the differences in the performance can be attributed to the higher membrane surface area per unit volume in the microchannel unit. The resulting elongated contact of H2O and H2 with the membrane layer magnifies the benefits of interchannel species transfer on the kinetics and thermodynamics of RWGS reaction discussed in Section 3.1. Increased geometric accessibility of the membrane is closely correlated with the stronger (more than one order of magnitude higher) volumetric intensification offered by the sub–millimeter structuring of the flow paths [29]. Combined with the advantage of facile scalability through numbering up strategy and the possibility of customizing syngas composition by careful selection of the operating conditions (Section 3.2), the findings in Fig. 8 point out membrane–integrated microreactor driven RWGS as an efficient route for syngas production at the desired scale.

### **4. Conclusions** 

Catalytic conversion of CO2 and H2 mixture to syngas _via_ RWGS is modeled isothermally in a membrane separation integrated microchannel reactor. The reactor model is shown to capture the literature–based experimental data successfully. Due to the permselectivity of the SOD membrane to H2O and H2, the proposed intensified reactor is configured in a way to maximize CO2 transformation by simultaneous transmembrane H2 dosing into and H2O removal from the reaction medium. The findings, all obtained at 523 K, show the effects of the operating conditions on per cent CO2 conversion, the molar amount of CO2 converted and syngas composition as summarized below: 

- Fluid velocities at the reaction and permeate channel inlets affect reactor performance significantly. CO2 conversion can be elevated at lower inlet velocities ( _i.e_ ., longer residence time) of the H2 + CO2 mixture and higher inlet velocities of the sweep gas (H2). These conditions favor the extent of cross–membrane H2 supply that promotes RWGS kinetically and thermodynamically. The promotion is magnified by the ejection of product H2O into the permeate side. 

- The maximum CO2 conversion of 52% is obtained at 15 bar, H2:CO2 = 3 at which the thermodynamic limit is 16.8%. This superior performance is observed at minimized molar dosing of the H2 + CO2 mixture, which undesirably limits the amount of CO2 converted and reduces the syngas production capacity of the multichannel reactor. Both performance metrics are positively correlated with reactor pressure and the inlet velocity ( _i.e_ ., molar flow rate) of the sweep gas. 

- • Membrane integration introduces significant flexibility in molar composition of syngas which changes in the 2.3–9.8 range. The composition decreases by reducing reactor pressure and increasing the inlet velocity of the reactive mixture. Imposing the recommended value of ~2 (for high temperature FT synthesis, methanol and DME production) points out the most favorable set of operating conditions that can deliver syngas composition of 2.7 ( _W/F_ CO2 _,_ in = 40 gcat h mol<sup>–1</sup> ; sweep gas inlet velocity = 6 × the pertinent reaction channel inlet velocity, 5 bar, H2:CO2 = 3). Under these conditions, CO2 conversion is 24.5% which exceeds ~16% of the membraneless case, and the amount of CO2 converted is 3.5 × 10<sup>–8</sup> mol s<sup>–1</sup> which is ~53% above of the membraneless case. 

- Syngas composition can be decreased further by reducing molar inlet H2:CO2 ratio. Despite a limited loss in CO2 conversion, decreasing H2:CO2 from 3 to 2 allows syngas production in a composition range of 1.7–2.8 at 5 and 15 bar, respectively. The conclusions stated so far for the co–current flow of the reactive mixture and sweep streams remain almost unchanged when the dosing is carried out in the counter–current mode. Nevertheless, the negligible impact of flow partitioning is valid for the current set of operating conditions and is likely to change in favor of the counter–current flow at higher reactor pressures. 

- Under equivalent operating conditions, the micro structured unit proposed herein can deliver ~2.5 times higher molar CO2 consumption per mass of catalyst and reactor volume and ~33% higher CO2 conversion than a packed–bed membrane reactor.
