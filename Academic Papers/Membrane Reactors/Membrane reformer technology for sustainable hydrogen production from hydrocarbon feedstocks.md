The reforming reactor configuration is one of the research areas where many efforts have been realized to achieve more sustainable production alternatives. In particular, the development of palladium (Pd)-based membrane reactors (MR) has gained significant attention in recent years. Pd-based alloys are mainly the membrane material of choice due to Pd superior H2 selectivity and permeance over a wide range of operating conditions, especially at higher temperatures usually needed for reforming processes [4,5]. The MR consists of a fixed-bed catalytic reactor housing an integrated Pd perm-selective membrane to facilitate H2 separation as it is being produced. In such reactors, the three main steps of the conventional SMR process, namely reforming, WGS, and H2 separation, are performed in a single unit. This results in process intensification and optimization in terms of the amount and size of equipment needed and associated capital expenditure. By removing H2 in-situ, greater CH4 conversions beyond the thermodynamic equilibrium can be achieved at milder conditions such as lower temperatures and less steam content [6–8]. Moreover, because the separation is driven by the high pressure of the feed, the retentate stream produced by the reactor is concentrated in CO2 and is at high pressure, allowing for carbon capture at reduced costs given that several stages of CO2 purification and compression would be avoided [7,9]. 

Several studies demonstrated both experimentally and numerically the achievable benefits from using MR over conventional reactors (CR). Lin et al. [10] demonstrated the equilibrium shift in CH4 conversions when integrating a 20 μm thick supported Pd membrane into the reactor. They showed that CH4 conversions above 80% were realizable at 500<sup>◦</sup> C and 9 bar in the Pd-based MR, whereas a temperature of 850<sup>◦</sup> C was needed to achieve the same conversion levels in a CR. Sheu et al. [3] conducted a numerical evaluation of the performance of a Pd–Ru MR for SMR in terms of CH4 conversion and H2 recovery at different operating parameters; 96.5% conversion and high H2 recovery was obtained at 550<sup>◦</sup> C and 3.5 bar. Lee et al. [11] performed a comparative numerical simulation of different MR systems with co-current and counter-current C and 600<sup>◦</sup> C. At flow configurations at temperatures between 400<sup>◦</sup> 600<sup>◦</sup> C, their results showed an enhancement in H2 production rate of about 42% and 49% when using a Pd–Ru based MR with co-current and counter-current sweep gas flows, respectively, compared to a CR. Moreover, they concluded that using a Pd–Ru based MR resulted in the highest H2 yield while a Pd–Ag based MR exhibited the best performance in terms of H2 purity. 

Iulianelli et al. [12] carried out an experimental investigation on a two-stage membrane-based system for H2 production and purification. They reported a CH4 conversion of 99% and 40% H2 recovery when operating a Pd–Ag MR at 400<sup>◦</sup> C and 2.5 bar with N2 sweep gas in the 1st stage, followed by 67% H2 recovery in a 2nd stage utilizing a Pd membrane for further purification, which summed to a total of 80% H2 recovery with 99.99% average purity. Moreover, Vigneault and Grace [13] conducted a proof-of-concept study of a multi-channel MR utilizing Pd–Ag membranes inside the reforming channels that were sprayed with Ru-based catalyst particles, and showed that CH4 conversions exceeding 91% and H2 purities of 99.99% and above were obtained at 570<sup>◦</sup> C and 15 bar. 

Fernandez et al. [14] evaluated the performance of a module con– taining five Pd Ag MRs under SMR conditions as a function of temperature and pressure, and reported conversions of 76% and 89% at 550<sup>◦</sup> C and 600<sup>◦</sup> C, respectively, with system pressures as low as 1.3 bar, all while maintaining H2 purity above 99.98% for all cases. A computational study of a Pd MR to enhance the membrane efficiency was performed by Shigarov et al. [15], where two MR configurations (tube in tube and sandwich-like) were systematically investigated. They reported that the tube-in-tube configuration showed better performance in terms of reduced transport limitations. However, changing the catalyst type from granular to closed-cross-flow structure coated with Ru-based 

catalyst has the potential to increase the H2 production rate by several fold. 

Barbieri et al. [16] presented a comprehensive analysis of a Pd-based MR used for SMR and discussed how the use of a MR improved the overall process performance. 

Sweeney et al. [17] performed an economic analysis of an industrial scale SMR process with a Pd-based MR, and their results showed that using an optimized MR process offered reductions in natural gas consumption and overall annual costs even with the higher capital cost usually associated with Pd membranes. Another techno-economic evaluation conducted by Lee and Lim [18] revealed that the levelized cost of H2 (LCOH) can be reduced by more than 36% when MR technology is used instead of a CR, as the LOCH dropped from $4 to $2.53 kg H−2 1. The cost savings are mostly attributed to the elimination of PSA units in the case of the MR, which reduces both capital and operating expenditures. 

Agnolin et al. [19] conducted a feasibility study on a Pd–Ag membrane supported on a Hastelloy-X filter for SMR using CH4 as a feed. The MR was packed with Ru-based reforming catalyst and was tested at temperatures ranging from 400<sup>◦</sup> C to 500<sup>◦</sup> C and retentate pressure of 5 bar. The realized CH4 conversions in MR exceeded the ones achieved in the CR at all tested temperatures, with maximum enhancement of nearly 60% achieved at 500<sup>◦</sup> C, as the conversion increased from 27% to 42%, while exceeding hydrogen purity of 99%. Kian et al. [20] tested a Pd-based MR with Ni-based commercial catalyst to determine the optimal operating conditions for highest CH4 conversion and H2 yield. Conversion of 42% was achieved at 400<sup>◦</sup> C and 4 bar, with ultra-pure H2 suitable for direct usage in fuel cell vehicles collected at the permeate side. Their study was expanded to investigate the competitive potential of coupling a MR system with carbon capture to produce high purity H2 with minimal CO2 emissions. It was reported that up to 80% of the produced CO2 can be captured when using a zeolite 13X packed bed downstream of the retentate stream. 

Kyriakides et al. [21] performed a model-based optimization of the –Ru based MR to operating conditions and the configuration of a Pd minimize the overall CH4 requirements for given H2 product specifications. They found that the overall CH4 utilization decreases asymptotically with increasing reactor length; increasing the reactor length from 0.25 m to 0.35 m resulted in a reduction in the CH4 feed required by about 67% for a H2 production rate of 0.047 m<sup>3</sup> h<sup>−1</sup> . 

The literature survey indicates that most of the previous studies were mainly focused on discussing the application of MR for SMR utilizing a single catalyst configuration and CH4 feed, with limited number of studies exploring other hydrocarbon feedstocks and integrated catalyst systems. Alrashed et al. [22] presented a comparative analysis of the – performance of Pd Au MR and commercial reforming catalyst (RK-400R, Topsøe) under high temperatures and pressures up to 625<sup>◦</sup> C and 40 bar, respectively, using CH4 and synthetic pre-reformed naphtha feeds, and found that higher conversions were obtained in the MR compared to a CR for both feeds under the different operating conditions. A follow-on numerical study [23] developed a mathematical model to evaluate the effect of other critical parameters, such as the membrane dimensions, concentration polarization, and inhibitive adsorption of co-existing gases on the MR performance. They reported that the pre-reformed naphtha feed achieved slightly higher CH4 conversion and H2 yield of 87% and 78%, respectively, at 650<sup>◦</sup> C and 40 bar, with an optimal membrane length of 0.2–0.3 m at 550<sup>◦</sup> C and 30 bar. It was also found that pre-reformed naphtha requires less heating load by 36% compared to CH4. Anzelmo et al. [24] conducted parametric and – long-term stability tests using a composite Pd Au MR packed with Ni-based reforming catalyst (HiFUEL® R110) to preform natural gas reforming under temperatures ranging from 350<sup>◦</sup> C to 450<sup>◦</sup> C and GHSV from 1300 h<sup>−1</sup> to 7800 h<sup>−1</sup> . They reported stable conversions of 32%, 90%, 89%, and 69%, for methane, ethane, propane, and butane, respectively when reforming was conducted at a temperature of 450<sup>◦</sup> C and GHSV of 2600 h<sup>−1</sup> .

They concluded that temperature was the most influential parameter on CH4 conversion, with conversions exceeding 99% achieved at 727<sup>◦</sup> C. 

Alongside integration of membranes in the reactor, utilization of multifunctional catalyst systems can aid in acquiring desired kinetics and further support process intensification objectives [26]. In this work, the focus is placed on demonstrating the Pd-based MR technology with a dual catalyst system for reforming and WGS as a means of maximizing conversion and H2 yield while minimizing CO as a by-product. The study also assesses the flexibility and feasibility of the technology in terms of the type of feed utilized though comparative analysis of different hydrocarbon feedstocks, namely CH4 and synthetic pre-reformed naphtha (PRN). This particular configuration is not widely investigated in literature, as most studies have looked into the application of MR either for reforming or WGS with their respective catalytic systems and conventional feeds. For such reasons, the performance of a combined mixture of SMR and WGS catalysts inside the MR were evaluated at industrially relevant operating conditions to further investigate the realizable advantages. 

# **2. Experimental method** 

– A composite Pd Au membrane was obtained from TNO for the purpose of this study, as shown in Fig. 1. 

The membrane was fabricated via electroless plating and electroplating techniques. First, the surface of a symmetric porous alumina ceramic tube (CoorsTek) was cleaned, activated, and then coated with a 

layer of Pd via electroless plating as described in Ref. [28]. The resulting Pd membrane was subsequently coated with a layer of Au via electroplating (TNO Energy Transition). The thickness of the Pd and Au layers were calculated to be 4.92 μm and 0.75 μm, respectively, based on the weight gain after deposition. The membrane had an outer diameter of 1.4 cm and an effective length of 15.1 cm, resulting in an active separation area of 66.4 cm<sup>2</sup> . The membrane specifications as reported by the manufacturer are summarized in Table 1. 

The Pd–Au membrane was placed in a tubular stainless-steel module (2.4 cm internal diameter), as shown in Fig. 2, which has been described in more detail in a previous publication [22]. The annular space between the Pd–Au membrane and the wall of the reactor shell was packed with 64 cm<sup>3</sup> (75.11 g) of a mixture of commercial catalysts (− 20 + 45 mesh size); low-temperature SMR catalyst (R-88(R)-HEAT-X™) and high-temperature WGS catalyst (SK-501 Flex™) at a ratio of 3:1 by volume. Both catalysts were supplied by Topsøe. The module was installed inside a vertical split four-zone electric furnace of a testing unit (AP Miniplant). A schematic representing the experimental set-up is shown in Fig. 3. A multipoint thermocouple was positioned inside the membrane near its centreline and kept at the testing temperature by regulating the furnace parameters. 

The feed compositions and test conditions are shown in Table 2. The gases used to make the simulated feed mixtures were CO, H2, CH4, and CO2, while N2 was used as a trace gas for the gas chromatography (GC) analysis. The gas flowrates were controlled via mass flow controllers. The flowrate of water was controlled by means of a high-pressure pump and the water was vaporized into steam in an electric evaporator unit before it was mixed with the other gases. The mixed stream of gases and steam was then heated to the reactor temperature in a pre-heater before it was fed to the catalyst bed of the MR. The permeate and retentate streams out of the reactor were passed through water-cooled condensers to condense unreacted steam and remove any moisture. After that, with the aid of a backpressure regulator, a slip stream of the dry retentate or permeate was directed to an online GC (Agilent 7890A) for composition analysis, and GC readings were registered regularly every 15–20 min until reaching steady state. A membrane sweep gas was not utilized in these tests. 

Alongside SMR testing, pure H2 permeation tests were conducted periodically to monitor the performance and stability of the membrane. The permeation tests were conducted using 2500–3000 cm<sup>3</sup> min<sup>−1</sup> (STP) pure H2 feed at 550<sup>◦</sup> C and 3 bar, while keeping the permeate side of the membrane at atmospheric pressure. 

Before initiating the testing in the MR module, experiments on the catalyst system (SMR + WGS) were conducted in a packed-bed reactor to evaluate the catalytic activity. The testing was conducted under conditions similar to the MR operating conditions, as shown in Table 3. The catalyst system was tested deliberately at higher GHSV than in the MR configuration, showing that equilibrium was reached at a higher GHSV, and thus providing more confidence in the assumption that equilibrium would be reached as well at the lower GHSVs used in the MR.

The main impurities in the permeated hydrogen for both feed cases were CO2 and CH4, as shown in Fig. 8. The amount of CO2 is almost double that of CH4 at all tested pressures and feeds, which is expected given that the retentate side contained higher concentrations of CO2 than CH4 at all conditions. No measurable levels of CO were detected; the lower detection limit of the GC for CO was ~0.2%. This result holds considerable significance if the produced H2 is to be used as a fuel in proton-exchange membrane fuel cell vehicles (PEM FCVs), given that CO is reported to have one of the greatest degrading effects on fuel cell performance compared to the other impurities usually found in H2 produced from hydrocarbons [38–40]. 

# _3.5. Hydrogen flux and permeance_ 

The H2 flux was measured periodically at 500–550<sup>◦</sup> C and 3 barg (1 bara permeate pressure) in between the reforming runs using pure H2 feed. The flux remained relatively stable between 0.15 and 0.165 mol m<sup>−2</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> over the course of daily reforming tests for more than 400 h on stream with no measurable loss of permeation as shown in Fig. 9, indicating the high stability and durability of the membrane under the conditions tested. This level of stability is crucial from process engineering and industrial deployment perspectives, as it ensures consistent and reliable performance in meeting the overall objectives for process productivity and efficiency. It should be noted that the quoted time on stream does not represent the full lifetime of the membrane as the testing was not terminated due to deterioration of performance. Nonetheless, – the usage of a Pd Au alloy instead of pure Pd for the membrane film contributes to achieving such levels of stability for longer periods of time, as pure Pd membranes showed a lifetime around 200 h when tested under industrial settings [41].

_n_ By finding the value of that results in the best linear fit between the flux and pressure differential, the permeance of the membrane can be determined from the slope of the fitted line as per Eq. (4). 

_n_ The best linear fit was obtained at value of 0.9 as reflected by the highest coefficient of determination R<sup>2</sup> shown in Fig. 10, which falls within the expected range 0.5 _< n <_ 1 for Pd membranes [42]. Permeation controlled by bulk diffusion will have an _n_ value of 0.5 and can be described by Sieverts’ law, while _n_ values approaching unity indicate greater influence of surface reactions on the permeation [43]. Thus, based on the obtained _n_ value, the H2 transport across the membrane is not governed by Sieverts’ law. This deviation indicates that at such membrane thickness, the path for bulk diffusion is not long enough for it to be the rate determining step in comparison to the mass resistance at the surface. Other potential contributing factors could be the presence of adsorbed contaminants on the membrane surface that can increase the activation energy barrier for H2 dissociative adsorption and combinative desorption, which consequently increases the surface mass transport resistance; or gaseous flow through defects at the membrane surface, which is reported to give exponents closer to 1 [44]. Nonetheless, the 

best linear fit gives a permeance of 2.69 × 10<sup>−6</sup> mol m<sup>−2</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> Pa<sup>−0.9</sup> , which is of the same order of magnitude of previously reported values for Pd composite membranes with similar thicknesses and pressure exponents [43]. 

The performance of the MR configuration tested in this work is benchmarked against the findings reported in prior research as shown in Table 4. Most of the prior studies have focused on reporting CH4 conversion and H2 permeance as the main performance parameters with limited data on CO and CO2 concentrations in the retentate stream. It is worth mentioning that in the studies that assume partial pressure exponents of 0.5, the permeance value is reported in the range of 10<sup>−4</sup> – 10<sup>−3</sup> mol m<sup>−2</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> Pa<sup>−0.5</sup> , while in those that report transport controlled by surface effects (n = 1), the permeance is usually in the range of 10<sup>−7</sup> – 10<sup>−6</sup> mol m<sup>−2</sup> s<sup>−1</sup> Pa<sup>−1</sup> , which is expected given that lower _n_ values result in steeper slopes for the same flux values as per Eq. (4). 

# **4. Conclusions** 

The results of lab-scale testing of the MR technology for SMR utilizing a dual catalyst system and two hydrocarbon feedstocks were presented and discussed. The tests were carried out using a Pd–Au composite membrane and a 3:1 mixture of SMR and WGS commercial catalysts in the MR. MN and PRN feeds were reformed at 550<sup>◦</sup> C and pressures ranging from 10 to 30 bar. The results showed that the MR exhibited excellent performance in terms of CH4 conversion and H2 production and purity. Relatively high CH4 conversions of 91% and 65% were achieved at 30 bar with MN and PRN feeds, respectively, with H2 purity exceeding 98.5% over the course of testing. The addition of WGS catalyst resulted in enhanced conversions and a significant reduction of the remaining CO content in the by-product stream, eliminating the need for WGS reactors downstream of the MR. Therefore, these results showcase both the potential and the flexibility of MR technology for sustainable H2 production from different hydrocarbon feedstocks in comparison to the thermodynamically-limited conventional packed-bed catalytic reactor process.
