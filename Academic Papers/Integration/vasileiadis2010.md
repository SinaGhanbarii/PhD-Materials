**Keywords** : alumina nanomembranes, membrane microreactor, reactor simulation, hydrogen separation, hydrogen fuel cells. 

**Abstract.** Our recent communication focuses on small scale and nanoscale type engineering applications of alumina inorganic membrane reactors and reactor-permeator systems for the conversion of renewable and non-renewable hydrocarbons and methane rich streams into hydrogen rich gas for direct inner application and operation of fuel cell systems. This study elaborates on new nanomembrane reactors for the steam-methane/hydrocarbon reforming and water gas shift reactions, including work in the synthesis, manufacturing, modeling and operation of such microreaction systems. The projected small scale reactors, separators and overall reaction systems are of current significance in the area of multifunctional microreactor and nanoreactor design and operation in connection with the operation of fuel cells for transportation, stationary, and portable power generation applications. An added advantage of such systems is the reactive and separative operations of the fuel cell membrane-processor which are combined to convert the hydrocarbon with steam to valuable fuel gas for continuous fuel cell operation. Moreover, the nanomembrane systems under development have the unique characteristics to perform multiple operations per unit volume, such as to utilize beneficial equilibrium shift principles in reactant conversion and product yield through the removal of permselective species (i.e., hydrogen) via the inorganic membrane out of the conversion/reaction zone. In this way, improved hydrogen and product yields can be achieved which exceed the equilibrium calculated yields. Simultaneously, the reaction products, such as synthesis gas (i.e., H2, CO and CO2) at the reactor exit can be used as fuel in mostly solid oxide and molten carbonate fuel cells. The role of the alumina nanomembrane is also in the main conversion and upgrading sections of these feedstocks in order to overcome existing heat and mass transfer limitations and increase the overall efficiency of the microreactor-fuel cell system. 

## **Introduction – Description of Materials** 

Utilization of improved microreactors and microreaction systems for hydrogen production and delivery in the fuel cell is of high importance for the optimized operation of the cell. Both low and high temperature fuel cells use hydrogen as fuel in the anode to produce electricity via electrochemical molecular hydrogen oxidation or decomposition reactions. We have reported on improved catalytic membrane reactors (also permreactors) using inorganic membranes for in-situ hydrogen and hydrogen-carbon oxide mixture separation and delivery in the cell [1-6]. We have examined methane steam reforming combined with water gas shift and paraffin hydrocarbon dehydrogenation reactions from a kinetic, catalytic and materials point of view [1-8]. Catalysis studies are also underway to produce improved catalysts for these reactions with minimal deactivation on stream. Our recent work is furthermore, on improved membranes and reactor configurations for hydrogen fuel delivery in the interconnected fuel cell. Variable composition feedstocks rich in methane (renewable and nonrenewable) are considered. Current microreactor designs utilize improved permselective and catalytic materials with different properties for achieving final desired syngas composition, purity and throughput. The proposed systems are applied to produce hydrogen rich fuel mixtures for continuous, reliable fuel-cell operation or direct hydrogen enhanced combustion both at improved overall efficiencies [1, 4, 6]. 

Specifically, our communication reports and reviews on the manufacturing and testing of membrane microreactors for hydrocarbon to hydrogen, steam reforming type reactions. The exit hydrogen mixtures are used to continuously power fuel cells. Part of the product reformed gas can be used in consecutive alternative chemical synthesis applications. As example, part of the H2, CO, CO2 rich product can be used for methanol synthesis as an alternative and efficient fuel [1-7]. 

The developed membrane microprocesses are of increased significance as well in the area of clean power generation with enhanced pollution minimization [13-15]. This includes reduction in CO2 emissions from hydrocarbon reforming plants via CO2 conversion in consecutive synthesis or recycling routes; also reduction of NOx emissions due to clean power generation from the fuel cell. We are working also on the unique characteristics of the developed operational membrane systems to perform multiple operations. Among others, to utilize the enhanced equilibrium shift in hydrocarbon conversion and hydrogen yield through the removal of hydrogen and other selective permeative gases out of the conversion zone and by the utilization of byproduct gases as chemical synthesis blocks [1-8]. 

Reduction in carbon deposition propensity on the catalyst is an excess requirement for such reforming microsystems, especially in view of converting higher hydrocarbons into hydrogen rich exit mixtures [9, 10, 12]. Nickel and nickel based catalysts enriched with earth type metals such as Ca, K, and La are again the starting point of our research for constant activity continuous operations. 

Advanced materials and membranes in microporous, nanoporous, or dense phases can be used in activated structures as hydrogen or hydrogen-carbon oxide receptors and transmitters in the above operations to recover hydrogen and hydrogen-carbon oxide mixtures directly out of the microreactor or from the post reacted exit streams. These include ceramics, metals, high temperature polymers, and special types of carbon based and composite materials [1, 5, 6].  Dense type materials are recent competitive candidates for specific hydrogen separations as the microporous ones. The rejected hydrocarbons and/or CO streams from a consecutive membrane permeator or separator can be recycled into the inlet of the reformer/gas-shift microreactor for increasing the process efficiency via the utilization of the unreacted gases. Product mixtures of H2 and CO2 from the permeate stream of the permeator, can be used directly as anode feed gas into an adjacent molten carbonate fuel cell unit [1-6].  Similarly, separated product mixtures of H2 and CO can be used directly as anode feed gas into an adjacent solid oxide fuel cell unit [1, 2, 4, 6].  In several reactor-catalyst-membrane instances the main microreactor operates at high enough conversions and produces hydrogen rich mixtures for direct usage in the fuel cell, thereby eliminating the need for the downstream separator/permeator and the associated costs [5-6].

## **Results and Discussion** 

Related modeling of such multifunctional microreactors and membrane systems for the aforementioned applications is under way to describe their function, operational conversion range, yield and capacity.  The models used, simulate relevant acquired data and predict conditions for best membrane reactor and process operation by variation of key intrinsic parameters [1, 5-7, 9, 11, 16]. The models utilize also kinetic data of the reactions (1) and (2) as these were reported earlier [1, 5, 9]. The proposed efficient membrane reactor-separator configurations and their specific fuel cellpower generation applications seek to perform multiple unit operations within a single or integrated module which makes them advanced in comparison with up to now proposed and utilized conventional and permeable type of reactors [1, 5, 9-12, 16, 17].  They project a promising advancing technology for primary and secondary hydrocarbon to hydrogen upgrading membranereactors for use in continuous (inline) fuel cell and power cycle processes [13-15]. The described positive technical aspects/effects are fortified in view of the environmental and economic benefits that these processes possess especially in connection with directly powering fuel cell stacks [2, 4, 6]. 

Figure 1 is the direct image of the layers that make the alumina membrane tube as they are shown through a SEM micrograph [5, 18]. The alumina based tube length is L=25.4cm, with an ID of 0.7 cm, and an OD of 1.0 cm [5, 18]. The inside top membrane layer is in the nanosize regime (4-5 nm) and is responsible for most of the particular gas diffusion and flow resistance. In this figure/scanning one can see also the characteristics and dimensions of all four layers including the support. The next to the top inner layer with a pore size of about 200 nm and a thickness of 15-30 µ m, is partly contributing to the total permselective ability (permeance) of the membrane tube including the higher selectivity for hydrogen. Overall, the gas permeation effect results in the higher separation of hydrogen via the membrane.
