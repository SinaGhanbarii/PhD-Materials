## **1. Introduction** 

The use of conventional fossil fuels has resulted in substantial greenhouse gas emissions, driving environmental problems like the greenhouse effect and global warming. Evidence increasingly shows that a global temperature rise exceeding 2<sup>◦</sup> C has already caused irreversible harm to the Earth [1]. Addressing these challenges requires the development of alternative energy sources and green, clean energy conversion technologies to reduce environmental pressures. Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFC) represent an innovative solution for converting the chemical energy of fuels into electricity. They offer numerous advantages, including energy conversion efficiencies of 60 % or higher, high-quality heat supply, cost-effective ceramic materials, nearly zero carbon emissions, and a compact installation footprint [2,3]. Furthermore, their high operating temperatures (600–900<sup>◦</sup> C) and porous nickel-based anode structure provide exceptional fuel flexibility compared to other fuel cell types [4]. SOFC can directly utilize hydrocarbon fuels like methane and methanol, significantly broadening their application scope. These features make SOFC particularly attractive for utility-scale, industrial, and distributed power generation. Despite these advantages, carbon deposition remains a significant challenge during SOFC operation. When hydrocarbons are introduced into the cell, the nickel catalyst in the anode breaks the C-H bonds in the hydrocarbons, leading to the formation of carbon nanofibers and soot [5]. This carbon accumulation blocks porous channels, impeding fuel gas diffusion and reducing the availability of active electrochemical reaction sites. Moreover, carbon deposition deactivates the nickel-based catalyst, hindering the reforming of hydrocarbons and limiting the production of hydrogen and carbon monoxide, which are critical for electrochemical reactions [6]. The presence of carbon also increases the anode’s internal resistance, causing higher ohmic losses and voltage drops. These effects collectively degrade SOFC performance, leading to reduced power output, lower energy conversion efficiency, and a shortened overall system lifespan. As a result, carbon deposition remains a significant barrier to the stable, long-term operation and commercialization of SOFC technology. 

Researchers have conducted extensive studies to address the carbon deposition problem in SOFC. One promising approach involves modifying the anode through replacement, doping, or surface alterations. Studies have investigated the effects of various anode materials, including Pd-, Cu-, Ni-, NiCu-BaZr0.1Ce0.7Y0.1Yb0.1O3-δ anodes [7], Sn-Ni/ScSZ SOFC [8], CeO2-modified Ni surfaces [9], and Ba (Ce0.9Y0.1)0.8Ni0.2O3–δ@CuO composites [10], on carbon deposition behavior. Additionally, a review by Zhang et al. [11] discusses carbon deposition processes, carbon detection methods, primarily focusing on alternative anode materials. However, these material and structural modifications present challenges related to technical feasibility, economic cost, and industrial scalability. A more practical and effective approach is to optimize operating conditions and adjust fuel composition to minimize carbon deposition. C-H-O ternary diagrams, as proposed by Sasaki et al. [12], Ribeiro et al. [13], and Jaworski et al. [14], allow prediction of carbon deposition likelihood on electrocatalyst surfaces by calculating equilibrium fuel compositions under thermodynamic conditions. These studies show that incorporating oxygen-containing components (e.g., CO2 or H2O) or hydrogen-containing components (e.g., H2O or H2) into the fuel can significantly reduce carbon deposition probability [15]. Besides, research by Lyu et al. [16] demonstrates that incorporating inert gases like N2 into the fuel can also help eliminate carbon deposition. Mermelstein et al. [17] compared Ni/YSZ (yttria-stabilized zirconia) and Ni/CGO (gadolinium-doped ceria) anodes using biomass gasification tars as fuel. Their results revealed that Ni/CGO anodes are more 

resistant to carbon formation, and the addition of steam to the fuel effectively reduces carbon deposition rates. In another study, Sakama et al. [18] developed multi-layer structured anodes with a detector layer to monitor carbon deposition near the electrolyte and electrode surfaces. They found that oxygen ions from electrochemical reactions and water as a reaction product play critical roles in suppressing carbon deposition. Bian et al. [19] conducted experiments using H2-CO mixture fuels and found no carbon deposition when pure CO was used. However, carbon formed in the presence of H2, indicating that H2 in the H2-CO mixture promotes carbon deposition. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing fuel composition and operating conditions to mitigate carbon formation in SOFC. Despite these advances, experimental studies face challenges in capturing key phenomena such as temperature distribution, gas flow within porous structures, carbon concentration, and reaction dynamics within the cell. Consequently, dynamic modeling and numerical simulations are of great significance for understanding carbon deposition behavior and its impact on SOFC performance degradation. 

Modeling and simulation offer robust, reliable, and cost-effective methods for analyzing complex physical phenomena within SOFC, allowing researchers to examine variables beyond the scope of experimental studies. Sciazko et al. [20] utilized a machine learning-assisted image processing framework to quantitatively reconstruct 3D structures of carbon deposition in SOFC electrodes. The reconstruction revealed that carbon layers formed over Ni particles, and the Ni pulverized due to high-temperature corrosion. At the same time, the YSZ network deformed due to internal stress. Wang et al. [21] employed the finite element method to simulate the two-dimensional concentration distribution of species, adjusting parameters such as the anode support layer’s pore structure, methane concentration in the fuel, and operating current density. Their findings indicated that reducing the input methane concentration and increasing the operating current density lowered the O/C ratio at the anode/electrolyte interface, thereby mitigating carbon deposition on Ni-based anodes. Mehrabian and Mahmoudimehr [22,23] developed a 3D numerical model to optimize the steam-to-carbon (S/C) ratio for SOFC fueled by biogas with varying methane content, aiming to prevent carbon deposition while maximizing power output. Furthermore, by integrating simulation results with artificial neural networks (ANN), they derived a mathematical correlation for the optimal S/C ratio as a function of operating temperature and biogas composition. This ANN-based correlation shows good agreement with the simulation results and can significantly reduce computation time by eliminating the need for iterative calculations. Corigliano and Fragiacomo [24] developed an ad hoc numerical modeling of SOFC directly fed by syngas of varying compositions based on the MATLAB environment. Their study revealed that feeding syngas with high methane content caused severe cracking issues, which proved difficult to manage by adjusting a single parameter. Li et al. [25] used a 2D model to evaluate cell performance and carbon deposition under various CH4-CO2 mixtures. They identified a CO2/CH4 molar ratio of 1 as optimal for achieving the best balance of power density, carbon activity, and methane conversion rate. Pongratz et al. [26] conducted carbon deposition risk assessments using a 2D model, concluding that steam gasification presents a higher risk for carbon deposition than air gasification. Zhu et al. [27] developed a 2D CFD model to examine SOFC performance over 140 days of operation under different conditions using natural gas as fuel. Their results showed that carbon deposition significantly reduced anode porosity (from 0.5 to 0.267) and reaction activity (from 1.0 to 0.45). Nouri et al. [28] used a 2D single-cell model to compare performance degradation in SOFC fueled by biogas and coal gasification syngas. They also investigated the effectiveness of adding

## _2.2.1. Heat and mass transfer Processes and electrochemical reaction models_ 

The main assumptions involved in the modeling process are as follows: (a) All gases in the model are treated as ideal gases; (b) Porous electrodes are considered isotropic and homogeneously permeable; (c) Gas flow is modeled as laminar due to the low Reynolds number; (d) Only radiation heat transfer between the outer surface and the environment is included; other radiation types are neglected [31]; (e) The model accounts for the methane decomposition (MD) and Boudouard reactions (BR) while ignoring the effects of other carbon formation reactions on the carbon deposition rate; (f) The electrochemical reaction of H2 is prioritized, as its reaction rate is 2–5 times faster than CO and contributes more than 98 % of the total current [32,33]. Therefore, only the electrochemical reaction of H2 is considered in this research.

Appropriate initial and boundary conditions are crucial for numerical computations. The bottom surface of the anode interconnector is grounded, while the top surface of the cathode interconnector is set to _U_ cell. All other external boundaries are insulated. ## _2.4. Model validation_ 

The governing equations of the thermodynamic model, mass transport, chemical reactions, and carbon deposition model were solved using the commercial software COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0, a finite element analyzer, solver, and simulation software package for various physics and engineering applications. The software discretizes mathematical models using the Finite Element Method (FEM), yielding corresponding numerical models. The discretized equations are then solved to obtain approximate solutions to specific problems. A detailed introduction to the FEM can be found in Ref. [44]. The entire computation process for solving fully coupled, dynamic, and nonlinear conservation equations was conducted through two consecutive simulations. Initially, the steady-state process without carbon deposition was computed. These steady-state results were then used as initial conditions for a time-dependent simulation over a subsequent 200-day period. The relative tolerance criteria of 1 × 10<sup>−5</sup> and 1 × 10<sup>−4</sup> were applied to assess the convergence of the steady-state and transient solutions, respectively. In this research, approximately 30 h were required to compute the results for a 200-day dynamic evolution process on a workstation running Windows 10, equipped with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6248R CPU @ 3.00 GHz. 

Comprehensive validation studies were conducted to evaluate the rationality of the model. As shown in Fig. 2 (a) and (b), the SOFC steadystate performance curves and the dynamic response results of current density calculated by the model were compared with the experimental results obtained by Lee et al. [30] and Bae et al. [45]. The comparison demonstrates that the simulation results are in good agreement with the experimental results. Since the output current density of the cell results from multiphysics coupling calculations, validation of the polarization curve confirms the reliability of the model established in this study. Carbon deposition within porous electrodes leads to a continuous decline in the output power of SOFC, eventually rendering the cell unable to meet electricity demands and necessitating its replacement. In this research, the working lifecycle of SOFC is defined as the period from its initial operation until its output power no longer satisfies electricity demand. For example, if the system’s initial power excess coefficient is 1.25, the SOFC will fail to meet electricity demand when its output power decreases to less than 0.8 times its initial output power (1/1.25 = 0.8). In this scenario, 0.8 represents the Power Degradation Threshold (PDT) of the cell, which is determined by the system’s initial power excess coefficient. The working lifecycle of the cell is thus the time span from the start of operation until its output power degrades to the PDT. During this period, the total power generated by the cell is referred to as its total working lifecycle power generation ( _P_ tot).

We calculated the cell’s working lifecycle power generation for PDT = 0.8 across different operating temperatures and methane molar fractions ( _X_ CH4), as illustrated in Fig. 8 (a). The results reveal a distinct _X_ CH4 boundary between high and low _P_ tot. When _X_ CH4 exceeds this boundary, the cell’s total power generation decreases markedly. At higher operating temperatures, this boundary occurs at approximately _X_ CH4 = 0.18. As the operating temperature decreases, the boundary shifts upward to _X_ CH4 = 0.25. Therefore, to ensure adequate power generation, the methane molar fraction should remain below 0.25. For operating temperatures exceeding 1010 K, the methane proportion should be limited to less than 0.18. When _X_ CH4 _<_ 0.12, increasing the operating temperature enhances the cell’s total power generation. However, at higher methane fractions, elevated operating temperatures reduce the cell’s working lifecycle, potentially lowering total power generation. 

Fig. 8 (b) illustrates the cell’s working lifecycle power generation for PDT = 0.8 under different output voltages and _X_ CH4. The results indicate that the power generation boundary shifts toward higher methane proportions as the output voltage increases. For _X_ CH4 _<_ 0.12, cells operating at a lower voltage (0.6 V) achieve the maximum power generation. When the methane proportion is between 0.12 and 0.15, cells at different voltages exhibit comparable power generation. In contrast, cells operating at higher voltages demonstrate slower power degradation and more stable output. When the methane proportion is between 0.15 and 0.25, increasing the output voltage effectively extends the cell’s working lifecycle, thereby increasing its total power generation. However, when the methane proportion exceeds 0.25, the cell’s power generation remains consistently low due to rapid power degradation. The analysis above suggests that for long-term SOFC operation, the output voltage should be selected based on fuel composition, and fuels with methane fractions exceeding 0.25 should be avoided. These findings provide theoretical guidance for determining optimal operating temperatures and output voltages for SOFC with varying fuel compositions. 

## **4. Conclusions** 

In this study, a 3D dynamic model of SOFC considering carbon deposition is developed to analyze the influence of key practical oper— ating conditions output voltage, operating temperature, and fuel composition—on the cell’s steady-state performance and long-term power degradation. The total working lifecycle power generation was evaluated for methane molar fractions ranging from 0.1 to 0.4, output voltages from 0.6 V to 0.8 V, and operating temperatures from 1003 K to 1043 K. The main findings are summarized as follows: 

- (1) Cells operating at lower output voltages exhibit higher steadystate current and power densities but experience faster carbon deposition and power degradation. At _τ_ = 200 days, the normalized output power at _U_ = 0.6V is 0.5, significantly lower than at higher voltages (0.73 for _U_ = 0.9V, 0.7 for _U_ = 0.8V). 

When PDT = 0.75, _P_ tot at _U_ = 0.75V reaches 2880 kW h/m<sup>2</sup> , approximately twice that at _U_ = 0.55V. Thus, the optimal output voltage for long-term SOFC operation should exceed the voltage _U_ = yielding maximum steady-state power density, specifically 0.7V–0.75V in this study. 

- (2) Increasing the operating temperature enhances steady-state power density but accelerates carbon deposition. The power degradation process can be divided into a rapid degradation stage and a slow degradation stage. The power degradation rate in the rapid degradation stage is significantly faster than that in the subsequent slow degradation stage. At _T_ = 1023K, the cell’s output power decreases by 30 % during the first 50 days but declines by only 10 % from 50 to 200 days. 

- (3) Reducing the operating temperature or decreasing the methane molar fraction in the feeding fuel via pre-reforming to regulate PDT to the slow degradation stage significantly increases the cell’s total working lifecycle power generation. When PDT = 0.75, as the operating temperature decreases from 1023 K to 1003 K, the cell’s _P_ tot jumps from 2880 kW h/m<sup>2</sup> to 9600 kW h/ m<sup>2</sup> . Similarly, As the methane proportion of the fuel decreases from 0.3 to 0.2, the cell’s _P_ tot jumps from 480 kW h/m<sup>2</sup> to 10560 kW h/m<sup>2</sup> . 

- (4) A distinct _X_ CH4 boundary exists between high and low _P_ tot. When _X_ CH4 exceeds this boundary, the cell’s total power generation decreases significantly. In practical operations, the appropriate fuel composition should be determined based on the output voltage and operating temperature. For fuels with high _X_ CH4, increasing the output voltage and reducing the operating temperature are recommended to mitigate power degradation.
