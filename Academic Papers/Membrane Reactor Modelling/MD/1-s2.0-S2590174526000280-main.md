Despite their potential, efuel production faces HydrogenMembrane reactor challenges such as high energy demand and low conversion efficiency. Membrane reactors, which integrate reaction and separation, offer a promising solution by improving yields and reducing energy requirements. This Sustainable energy production review presents a scientometric analysis of membrane reactors for efuel production using the Scopus database Synthetic fuels from 2003 to 2024. Analyzing 30 publications, six thematic clusters were identified using VOSviewer and Bibliometrix. Keyword co-occurrence and factorial analyses highlight main research themes and emerging areas, revealing gaps in reactor configuration optimization. Influential studies show that membrane reactors can enhance CO2 conversion and methane yield compared to conventional systems, though challenges remain in membrane selectivity, economic viability, and long-term durability under real feedstock conditions. Additional issues include scalable module manufacturing and the lack of harmonized techno-economic, life cycle, and performance metrics. Sector-specific analysis identifies positive dynamics, such as compatibility with existing infrastructure, improved energy security, and supportive policies, as well as negative dynamics, including high production costs, resource competition, technological uncertainties, and new safety and regulatory requirements. By mapping research progress, this study provides insights to guide the advancement of membrane reactors and support sustainable efuel production and decarbonization goals. 

### **1. Introduction** 

The global energy landscape is changing rapidly due to the need to address environmental damage and ensure a steady supply of energy for a growing population. Rising greenhouse gas emissions, mainly from burning fossil fuels, have made climate-related issues worse, such as rising sea levels, ecosystem disruption, and extreme weather events. International agreements, like the 2015 Paris Agreement, emphasize the importance of limiting global warming by shifting to low-carbon energy systems. Many countries have increased their renewable energy capacity, and projections show a significant increase in clean energy contributions over the next decade [1]. In 2024 alone, a record 585 GW of renewable power capacity was added globally, meaning renewables 

accounted for over 90% of all new power capacity expansions during the year, by solar and wind energy. The International Energy Agency estimates that renewables’ share of global electricity generation could rise from about 32% in 2024 to roughly 43% by 2030. Despite this momentum, significant regional disparities remain, and the current pace of growth is still insufficient to meet the international goal of tripling renewable capacity by 2030 [2,3]. While renewable sources are crucial for reducing dependence on fossil fuels, their inconsistent availability poses challenges for maintaining grid stability and ensuring a constant energy supply. This calls for advanced technologies in energy storage and conversion [4]. 

A key sector contributing to emissions is transportation, which in 2022 accounted for 21.6% of global carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions

The sector’s slow adoption of renewable energy, coupled with its reliance on existing infrastructure, underscores the need for sustainable fuel alternatives that can integrate seamlessly without requiring extensive infrastructural changes. Power-to-X (PtX) technologies can convert renewable electricity into chemical energy carriers, such as electrofuels (efuels) and biofuels, which can decarbonize transportation while using current systems. Research identifies three primary strategies for this transition: (i) direct electrification through battery-powered vehicles, (ii) hydrogen-powered vehicles using indirect electrification, and (iii) non-fossil fuels like efuels for conventional internal combustion engines [5,6]. 

Green hydrogen, made through electrolysis powered by renewables, is at the center of PtX. It can be used directly or turned into synthetic chemicals. However, global electrolysis capacity, while growing fast, still faces limitations due to hydrogen’s low energy density, storage problems, and safety risks like corrosion and leaks [7]. This storage requirement is necessary due to the intermittency of renewables. For instance, a 100 MW solar facility may generate a significant energy surplus (30–50% above immediate demand), which must be stored as hydrogen to ensure electrolyzer utilization and economic viability [8]. The resulting hydrogen must then be stored for durations ranging from hours for grid balancing to months for seasonal energy shifting, each application imposing distinct technical demands [8]. Common storage methods face a critical trilemma between energy density, efficiency, and cost. For example, while liquid hydrogen offers high density (~8.5–10 MJ/L), its round-trip efficiency is only 55–65% due to energy-intensive liquefaction [9]. It is these inherent limitations that create a compelling rationale for converting hydrogen into chemically stable carriers like e- ammonia or e-methanol. These efuels offer higher volumetric energy densities, can use existing global infrastructure, and often present a net efficiency advantage when conversion losses are compared to the combined storage and transport losses of pure hydrogen [10]. For example, even when compressed to 700 bar, hydrogen’s volumetric energy density (~5.6 MJ/L) remains an order of magnitude lower than that of liquid fuels like gasoline or diesel (see Table A1). To solve these issues, efuels such as e-methane, e-kerosene, e-ammonia, and e-methanol are getting more attention. They store more energy, work with today’s infrastructure, and are easier to transport. These fuels come from green hydrogen and capture CO2, giving a way to cut emissions in sectors that are slow to switch to electricity, like aviation, shipping, and longdistance road transport. Unlike biofuels, which need biomass, efuels are more flexible. They can reduce emissions and use existing infrastructure while pairing renewable electricity with CO2 capture [11]. 

The production of efuels, however, faces several technical and environmental barriers. Traditional synthesis processes, often reliant on H2 derived from fossil-based methane (CH4) steam reforming, generate considerable CO2 emissions, undermining their sustainability. For example, synthesizing ammonia requires high-pressure and hightemperature conditions (typically 400-500<sup>◦</sup> C and 100–200 bar) using iron or ruthenium catalysts, while methanol production operates at 200300<sup>◦</sup> C and 50–100 bar with copper-based catalysts [12]. FischerTropsch synthesis, used for producing hydrocarbons, i.e., e-kerosene, operates under similarly demanding conditions, with low conversion efficiency and challenges in utilizing CO2 directly due to catalyst deactivation from water by-products [13]. Hydrogen recovery from ammonia decomposition or methanol reforming further complicates the process, requiring high temperatures (e.g., 700<sup>◦</sup> C for ammonia cracking) and energy-intensive purification methods such as pressure swing adsorption [14]. These inefficiencies highlight the need for advanced technologies that optimize reaction conditions, reduce energy consumption, and enhance scalability for sustainable efuel production. 

Membrane reactors are an important technology for efuel synthesis. They combine reaction and separation in one unit and address key limits of conventional processes. First, they shift reaction equilibrium by selectively removing products such as water, which increases single-pass 

conversion beyond thermodynamic limits [15]. Second, they improve selectivity and protect catalysts by controlling reactant supply or removing inhibitors, which stabilizes yields and extends catalyst lifetime [16]. Third, they intensify the process by combining several unit operations into one system. This reduces energy use, equipment size, and system complexity [17]. These features make membrane reactors suitable for efficient and scalable efuel production in PtX systems. In practice, selective membranes embedded in catalyst-filled reactors enable product removal through pressure-driven transport. This allows higher yields under milder conditions and reduces side reactions and catalyst deactivation. Membrane reactors can also function as reactant distributors or as contactors for selective product extraction [18]. These features make them suitable for compact and flexible applications, including maritime systems and off-grid power. Despite these advantages, technical challenges still limit large-scale deployment, and continued research is needed to support industrial adoption and reduce reliance on fossil fuels. 

Since the topic of efuel production is currently of significant relevance, as evidenced by the themes of research, several studies have been conducted based on membrane reactor technologies and efuel production processes [6,19,20]. Yet their combined application remains underexplored, with no prior studies utilizing scientometric analysis to evaluate the progress of research in this integrated field. As a result, this paper presents the following contributions: 

- Conducts a scientometric assessment (2003–2024) to map the research landscape, identifying seminal publications and tracing the evolution of membrane reactor applications in efuel production. 

- Characterizes international collaborative networks and delineates emerging methodological approaches through keyword cooccurrence and thematic mapping. 

- • Provides a sector-specific critical analysis that outlines key positive and negative dynamics, including infrastructure, production costs, and policy support, with practical insights for shifting from conventional fuels to sustainable efuels. 

To guide the systematic assessment of research progress and thematic evolution, this study addresses the following research questions (RQs): 

**RQ1:** What are the main publication trends over the past two decades, including growth over time, international collaboration, and leading journals? 

**RQ2:** What are the main research themes and topic clusters in this field based on keyword analysis and thematic mapping? 

**RQ3:** Which articles, authors, and countries have had the greatest impact on this research area, and how do they collaborate? 

**RQ4:** What membrane reactor technologies are used to produce different efuels, and what are their performance results and challenges? 

**RQ5:** What are the key opportunities and barriers in moving from conventional fuels to efuels, and what practical lessons can industry and policymakers learn? 

This paper is structured into six sections: Section 2 details the types of efuels; Section 3 describes the methodology; Section 4 presents the results and analysis; Section 5 provides a discussion, and Section 6 concludes the study. 

### **2. Types of efuel** 

The diverse spectrum of efuels presents promising opportunities for advancing sustainable energy systems, particularly within sectors that are challenging to electrify. Among the various efuels, e-methane, e- methanol, e-ammonia, and e-diesel stand out due to their potential scalability, versatility, and ability to integrate into existing infrastructure. Each of these fuels offers unique production pathways, applications, and challenges. This section explores these efuels in detail.

### _2.1. E-methane_ 

E-methane, a synthetic form of CH4 produced through the methanation process, is an alternative to natural gas. This compound appears when H2, ideally sourced from renewable energy systems, reacts with CO2 in a controlled chemical reaction. The process begins with electrolysis, where water is split into H2 and oxygen (O2) using renewable electricity, followed by the introduction of this H2 into a methanation reactor. Here, it combines CO2 at temperatures ranging from 250 to 400<sup>◦</sup> C and pressures of 5 to 50 bar, following the Sabatier reaction [21]. 

### CO2 + 4H2→ CH4 + 2H2O 

This method requires approximately 5.5 kg of CO2 for every kg of H2, a demand that often necessitates transportation from carbon capture and storage facilities, thereby increasing logistical costs and complexity [22]. The resulting e-methane can be integrated into existing natural gas grids that will provide a versatile fuel for heating, electricity generation, and powering natural gas vehicles. A prime example of this technology in action is the Audi e-gas project in Germany, recognized as the world’s largest power-to-gas facility. Operational since 2013, it produces about 1000 tons of synthetic CH4 annually, utilizing CO2 from a biogas plant and distributing the output through established infrastructure to compressed natural gas refueling stations [23]. This initiative not only demonstrates the feasibility of storing renewable energy in chemical form but also shows e-methane’s potential to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions when production relies entirely on renewable power sources.

Crucially, about 98% of global ammonia is currently derived from fossil fuels like natural gas and coal, a process that contributes around 1.8% to worldwide CO2 emissions [29,30]. The traditional Haber-Bosch process drives this production, combining nitrogen and H2 at temperatures of 400–450<sup>◦</sup> C and pressures of 150–200 bar, utilizing an iron-based catalyst to achieve a conversion rate of 25–35% per cycle. 

In contrast, e-ammonia is produced using green hydrogen generated from renewable energy sources, offering a carbon-free alternative. This shift, however, introduces environmental considerations, including the risk of NOx emissions and potential ecological harm from ammonia leaks into soil, air, and water, which can lead to biodiversity loss and eutrophication [31]. As a fuel, e-ammonia powers gas turbines and internal combustion engines, often requiring thermal cracking at 400600<sup>◦</sup> C to manage its high ignition temperature, sometimes with a combustion promoter like diesel or H2 derived from onboard reforming [32]. The Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organization in Australia has pioneered its use in fuel cells, equipping two specially designed vehicles with membrane technology that separates H2 from ammonia for clean energy output [33]. Stored as a liquid at a modest pressure of 1 MPa, e-ammonia benefits from existing distribution networks, enhancing its practicality. A notable development is the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company’s planned blue ammonia plant in Ruwais, United Arab Emirates, with a capacity of 1000 kilotons per year, underscoring its potential as a zero-carbon fuel through carbon capture and storage integration [34].

### _2.4. E-diesel_ 

E-methanol (CH3OH) is a versatile alcohol with potential as both a fuel and a building block for industrial chemical production, traditionally derived from fossil resources such as natural gas and coal. Global production currently stands at 98 million tons per year, releasing approximately 0.3 gigatons of CO2 annually, which constitutes about 10% of the chemical sector’s emissions. Forecasts predict a surge in demand to 500 million tons by 2050, potentially escalating emissions to 1.5 gigatons yearly if fossil fuel dependency continues unabated [24]. In contrast, e-methanol offers a sustainable alternative, synthesized from renewable feedstocks like biomass or captured CO2, paired with green hydrogen generated through renewable energy sources [25]. The production process relies on catalytic hydrogenation, where CO2 and H2 in a 1:3 ratio react within a reactor at 200–300<sup>◦</sup> C and 50–100 bar, employing Cu/Zn/Al-based catalysts to facilitate the reaction [26]. 

### CO2 + 3H2→ CH3OH + H2O 

An emerging technique involves converting e-methane directly into e- methanol using water as an oxidizing agent and a copper-zeolite catalyst, though this method remains experimental, with industrial application likely 5 to 20 years away [27]. E-methanol’s energy density, roughly half that of gasoline, suits it for use in modified spark-ignition engines, while its chemical properties support the manufacture of products like formaldehyde and dimethyl ether (DME). China leads global adoption, establishing standards for M15 and M85 fuel blends containing 15% and 85% methanol, respectively, and pilot projects such as the MefCO2 plant in Germany, which produces 500 tons annually using CO2 from coal-fired plants, illustrate its practical implementation [28]. This dual role positions e-methanol as a key player in decarbonizing transportation and industrial processes.

E-diesel is a synthetic diesel made using power-to-liquid technology, which helps create sustainable fuels that can be used with current infrastructure. This fuel is synthesized using the Fischer-Tropsch process, where H2 and carbon monoxide (CO) react at temperatures between 190 and 350<sup>◦</sup> C and pressures of 20 to 50 bar, catalyzed by iron or cobalt materials to form liquid hydrocarbons [35,36]. The process begins with the generation of syngas, either from CO2 and H2 via the reverse water–gas shift reaction or directly through co-electrolysis, followed by conversion into diesel-range hydrocarbons (C12–C22) [37]. Alternatively, e-diesel can be derived from methanol intermediates through the methanol-to-gasoline (MTG) pathway, involving sequential reactions starting with DME synthesis, olefin formation, and hydrotreating to refine the product [38]. ### 2CH3OH → CH3 − O − CH3 + H2O 

The choice of catalyst is critical: iron-based catalysts, favored for their cost-effectiveness and flexibility with syngas ratios (0.5–2.5 H2/CO), suit high-temperature operations, while cobalt-based catalysts, requiring lower temperatures (190–240<sup>◦</sup> C) and a narrower ratio (2.0–2.3), enhance diesel yield [39]. E-diesel’s chemical similarity to fossil diesel allows it to function as a drop-in fuel, requiring no engine modifications and offering the advantage of being sulfur-free, a trait that distinguishes it from biodiesel esters [40]. Companies such as Neste and Carbon Clean Solutions, in collaboration with LanzaTech, are driving its development, utilizing its compatibility with current refueling networks to support a transition to cleaner energy [41]. With an efficiency factor of 0.82 to 0.83, e-diesel’s sustainability hinges on renewable raw material sources, positioning it as a promising solution for decarbonizing transportation.

Combustion of e-diesel releases CO2, and climate benefits depend on a closed carbon loop based on renewable electricity and captured CO2. Ongoing research on advanced catalysts, such as Fe–Mn–K systems, aims to improve conversion efficiency and selectivity toward longer hydrocarbon chains, which could enhance the environmental performance of e-diesel [44]. 

### _2.5. E-kerosene_ 

E-kerosene is a synthetic aviation fuel made using power-to-liquid technologies, providing a practical solution to lower the carbon emissions from air travel, which contributes 2–3% of global emissions. This fuel is produced via the Fischer-Tropsch process, where H2 and CO, derived from CO2 and H2 or syngas, react under controlled conditions of 190 to 350<sup>◦</sup> C and 20 to 50 bar, guided by cobalt or iron catalysts to yield hydrocarbons in the C8–C16 range suitable for aviation [36]. For production, the process sequentially includes the compression of H2 and CO2, the generation of CO via the reverse water–gas shift, and then Fischer-Tropsch synthesis. After that, hydrocracking is performed to break long chains into kerosene-compatible molecules. This step enhances the quality, and as a result, its standard aligns with sustainable aviation fuel (SAF), allowing it to blend with conventional fuels like Jet A-1 or JP-8 without the need for aircraft modifications [45]. This process requires approximately 1.36 kg of H2 and 14.55 kg of CO2 per kg of SAF [25]. Leading companies such as SkyNRG, Neste, and LanzaTech are working on their development, while airlines like Lufthansa have successfully conducted test flights. As of 2021, Germany stands out as a key producer, aiming to expand usage to meet growing demand for cleaner aviation options [41]. 

The adoption of e-kerosene carries notable implications for the aviation industry’s sustainability goals, offering a means to decarbonize a hard-to-electrify sector. Its compatibility with existing infrastructure and engines facilitates gradual integration, with test flights demonstrating its reliability, e.g., a Boeing 737 flight from Amsterdam to Madrid in 2021 used 500 L of e-kerosene blended with traditional fuel [46]. However, production remains limited, constrained by the complexity of feedstock composition and the small scale relative to conventional aviation fuel volumes [47]. Research continues to refine catalyst performance, with Fe–Mn–K catalysts showing promise for higher selectivity toward aviation-grade hydrocarbons. Economic feasibility hinges on H2 costs ($0.8–$5 per kg) and CO2 pricing ($0 to $76.2 per ton), with the minimum fuel selling price ranging from $4.30 to $6.78 per gallon [48]. 

### _2.6. E-gasoline_ 

E-gasoline, another product of power-to-liquid technologies, is a synthetic alternative to conventional gasoline, addressing the need for 

renewable fuels in the transportation sector. It is synthesized through the Fischer-Tropsch process or the MTG route, where H2 and CO react at 190 to 350<sup>◦</sup> C and 20 to 50 bar, catalyzed by iron-based materials to produce hydrocarbons in the C4–C12 range [35]. The MTG process involves converting methanol into DME, followed by olefin synthesis and oligomerization, with hydrotreating to finalize the gasoline product [38]. Iron catalysts, operating at higher temperatures (320–350<sup>◦</sup> C), are effective for this shorter-chain output, offering flexibility with syngas ratios (0.5–2.5 H2/CO), though cobalt catalysts can also be used at lower temperatures (190–270<sup>◦</sup> C) for refined selectivity. E-gasoline’s chemical properties align with traditional gasoline, enabling its use in existing spark-ignition engines without modification, a key advantage for rapid adoption. Companies such as ExxonMobil and Haldor Topsoe, through the TIGAS process, have scaled this technology, with a 2011 Chinese plant achieving a 3000-ton daily capacity [49]. Its production efficiency, though lower than some efuels (0.82–0.83), depends on renewable H2 and CO2 availability. 

Environmental impact and life-cycle performance of e-gasoline depend on the production pathway. E-gasoline can achieve large greenhouse gas reductions when renewable electricity and biogenic or atmospheric CO2 are used. Under these conditions, carbon intensity can fall below 20 gCO2-eq per MJ, which represents more than an 85% reduction compared to fossil gasoline. These benefits decrease quickly if electricity comes from carbon-intensive grids or if CO2 is linked to fossil sources. Energy efficiency is a major challenge. Electrolysis operates at about 65% to 70% efficiency, and fuel synthesis operates at about 65% to 85% efficiency. Overall electricity-to-fuel efficiency ranges from 40% to 60%, which requires much more renewable electricity than direct electrification [50]. Large-scale deployment also stresses CO2 supply chains. About 82 g of CO2 is required per MJ of fuel, which increases energy use and infrastructure needs for capture, purification, and transport Combustion of e-gasoline can reduce some pollutants such as hydrocarbons and CO, but it does not eliminate NOx and particulate emissions [51]. In some cases, aromatic-related emissions may increase depending on fuel blend and engine design. Therefore, e-gasoline is best suited as a transitional drop-in fuel for existing vehicles, and its environmental benefit depends on low-carbon electricity, sustainable CO2 sources, and use in sectors where electrification is difficult [52]. ### **3. Methodology** 

This study employed a scientometric analysis to map research trends and identify themes at the intersection of membrane reactors and efuel production. Its reliance on quantitative methodologies helps to eliminate bias in systematic literature reviews. The bibliometric information was obtained from the Scopus database, which contains scholarly articles that aggregate pertinent research and offer unbiased summaries of findings. As one of the largest citation and abstract databases, it includes a wide range of peer-reviewed articles. It also provides useful tools for analysis, such as tracking citations, calculating the h-index, and identifying research trends. A systematic search method was applied on 16th August 2025 using Boolean operators to create specific queries. The OR operator was used to connect related keywords, AND linked different sets of keywords, and the NOT operator helped exclude unwanted terms. The query was set up as: (TITLE-ABS-KEY((“membrane reactor*” OR “membrane technology*” OR “membrane process*”) AND (“efuel production” OR “efuel processing” OR “electrofuels” OR “synthetic fuels” OR “power-to-liquid” OR “power-to-gas”)) AND NOT TITLE (review)).

### _4.3. A brief implication_ 

The transition to efuels will happen slowly if challenges are handled well. Stakeholders must manage economic pressures, technological risks, and uncertainties in the current system. The future of the sector depends on creating smart governance and reliable technologies to overcome obstacles. Without progress, efuels could turn into an expensive experiment with little effect. A practical approach is needed to navigate this complex landscape and plan for realistic outcomes. Several actionable steps can be taken, including: 

- Develop sector-specific regulatory frameworks to standardize efuel specifications. This ensures that engine manufacturers in aviation and shipowners in maritime shipping follow consistent safety and performance criteria. 

- Establish public–private partnerships to fund infrastructure upgrades, such as grid enhancements for steel plants and water management systems for road transportation projects. 

- Implement community engagement programs in regions impacted by land use, particularly agricultural areas near renewable energy sites for road and aviation projects. Transparent negotiations can address disputes and secure local support. 

- Create training initiatives for maritime crews and chemical plant staff to handle toxic substances like e-ammonia and optimize e- methanol production. 

- Launch pilot programs to test efuel technologies across all sectors. This will allow stakeholders to identify and resolve technical issues before full-scale implementation. 

- Coordinate international forums to align subsidy policies and investment strategies. This will prevent market distortion in the chemicals sector and ensure fair support for efuel adoption across different regions. 

### **5. Discussions** 

Using membrane reactors in PtX systems for efuel production is an important step toward cleaner energy. However, this change brings technical, economic, and operational challenges. Membranes, especially those made from precious metals like palladium, are essential for producing high-purity hydrogen. They can suffer from thermal stress, chemical damage, and fouling, which shortens their lifespan and raises costs. Adding other metals can reduce hydrogen embrittlement, but it often lowers the membranes’ ability to separate hydrogen effectively. This creates a trade-off between durability and efficiency. Ceramic membranes cost less, but they have trouble keeping hydrogen very pure, which limits their use in some efuel processes. Producing efuels like ammonia and hydrocarbons requires a lot of energy. This high energy use can make efuels less environmentally friendly than direct electrification. Membrane reactors help increase conversion rates, but keeping the right pressure for separation and controlling heat in smaller systems uses a lot of energy, which reduces efficiency. Integration with fluctuating renewable energy complicates reactor control, directly affecting critical operational variables. Variations in H2 feed rate, inlet pressure, and temperature disrupt stoichiometric ratios (e.g., H2/CO2), shifting reaction equilibria and conversion efficiency. Exothermic syntheses amplify these fluctuations into unstable thermal profiles and hot-spot formation, requiring precise regulation of coolant flows. Simultaneously, the permeation driving force across the membrane becomes unstable, altering separation performance and product purity. This tight coupling of feed variability, thermal inertia, and separation dynamics makes consistent reactor performance under renewable intermittency a significant control challenge. Financial issues also make adopting efuels difficult. High upfront costs for materials, complex reactor designs, and the expense of producing green hydrogen and capturing CO2 are major barriers. Large investments and retrofitting old plants remain challenges, especially since clear performance standards and regulations are not yet in place. In addition, integrating membrane reactors with renewable energy systems is complicated due to the fluctuating energy inputs. This variation makes it hard to control the process efficiently, and maintaining consistent performance is still a work in progress, with advanced monitoring and control strategies still under development. 

Despite these challenges, membrane reactors have strong potential for efuel production. Hybrid composites that mix metal and ceramic materials are being developed to improve stability while keeping high hydrogen purity. This approach could reduce the need for expensive precious metals and lower production costs. Silica-based and zeolite membranes are also being studied to perform better at high temperatures, making e-methane and e-methanol production more efficient. New reactor designs combine reaction, separation, and heat exchange to save energy and increase output. Modular reactors for decentralized applications, such as maritime or off-grid systems, provide practical ways to produce efuel locally without major changes to infrastructure. 

From an economic point of view, membrane reactors could lower operating costs and improve efficiency. Future developments, like selfhealing membranes, may extend their lifespan and reduce maintenance expenses. Technologies such as artificial intelligence and machine learning could help optimize reactor conditions in real time, boosting output and cutting energy use. Modular, plug-and-play systems could
