#### 1. Introduction 

Fuel cells are becoming more and more popular as they are high efficient energy conversion devices, which will aid in efforts to lower global greenhouse emissions [1,2]. Solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs), in particular, present a promising opportunity to efficiently convert the fuel energy of common fuels. Their high operating temperatures between 600<sup>�</sup> C and 1000<sup>�</sup> C mean that they have a high fuel flexibility in addition to providing high quality waste heat [3]. These benefits have opened up markets for both, stationary and mobile applications, wherein SOFC stacks are applied to auxiliary power units (APUs) [4]. 

The high fuel flexibility has prompted researchers to operate SOFCs with a variety of fuel compositions containing carbonaceous gases, which has already proven to be an important topic in SOFC literature. The gas mixtures that have been used include, for instance, the products of reformate and biomass gasification processes [5e16]. All of these synthetic mixed gas compositions

Investigations of SOFC operation with carbon monoxide as single reactant species in a fuel have been documented less often in the literature [3,11,17e22], advanced investigations by means of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) have so far been few and far between. Lebtron et al. [11] examined the role of CO and CO2 in syngas generated by biomass gasification processes and for this reason they added 3 vol% steam to most of the investigated gas compositions. They proved that carbon oxides had no damaging influence on anode supported cells with Ni=YSZ anodes at operating temperatures between 750<sup>�</sup> Ce800<sup>�</sup> C. Using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, the authors also showed that mass transfer is the limiting factor for operation with higher CO amounts than 27 vol% in CO=N2 mixtures. Jiang and Virkar [18] compared SOFC operation with varying binary fuel mixture combinations on SOFCs with Ni=YSZ anodes and YSZ=SDC bilayer thin-film electrolytes. The gases they used for their investigations were CO, CO2, H2, H2O and He. They achieved worse electrochemical performance with CO=CO2 mixtures than with gas compositions containing H2 as a result of the slower electrochemical oxidation of CO as well as the higher anodic concentration polarization. Sumi et al. [21] presented a 20 h test on the Ni=YSZ of both anode- and electrolyte-supported button cells supplied with CO/ CO2/N2 ¼ 25/5/70 vol% at 650<sup>�</sup> C. After the experiments, they recognized that carbon deposits from CO were evident on the surface of the anodes. At the interface between electrolyte and anode only small amounts of carbon were found. In their study, Li et al. [20] explained the carbon deposition characteristics of CO=CO2 fuel on SOFC button cells with Ni=YSZ anodes at a cell voltage of 0.7 V. The amount of carbon deposited was mainly dependent on the polarization time, operating temperature and CO mole fraction. The carbon formed primarily on the Ni catalyst and the structure was regular graphitic and rarely amorphous. AlzateRestrepo et al. [23] compared carbon formations resulting from different CO=H2 gas compositions at an operating temperature of 800<sup>�</sup> C. Smaller amounts of carbon formed as they decreased the volume fraction of hydrogen. Cell operation using pure carbon monoxide however, did not result in carbon deposits in their experiments. 

This work focuses on the investigation of large planar SOFCs (100 mm � 100 mm) operated with gas mixture combinations of CO, CO2, H2 and H2O in N2. First, performance with gas mixtures containing CO=CO2=N2 was evaluated, during which measurements of H2=H2O=N2 mixtures were carried out as benchmark tests in order to quantify the cell's performance. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements were applied in order to specify the loss mechanisms. Further, degradation due to CO fuelling was also comprehensively investigated. CO=H2=CO2=N2 gas mixtures were used to create carbon formations at 800<sup>�</sup> C. The long term stability of CO=CO2=N2 fuels at 700<sup>�</sup> C is also examined, and their influence on the cell's microstructure is presented. To the best of our knowledge, such detailed advanced experimental investigations of large planar solid oxide single cells operated with CO=CO2=N2 and CO=H2=CO2=N2 gas compositions have yet to be documented in literature. 



#### 2. Experimental setup 



Other works that examine stability and degradation behavior under special circumstances focus on CO-containing fuel composition for Ni=YSZ anodes. The most common degradation processes are given by Khan et al. [24] in their review of degradation mechanisms of Ni=YSZ anodes. To avoid carbon deposits from carbonaceous fuels are thereby an important criteria that has to be considered. Solid carbon is likely to form when SOFCs are operated with carbonaceous fuels, depending on the temperature, fuel composition, and the steam content in the fuel [13,25]. These solid carbon deposits cover the active sites of the anode, impede electrochemical reactions, and block gas channels [8,24]. Furthermore carbon dissolution in Ni is possible, what leads to significant enlargements of the nickel particles [23,26]. Despite carbon depositions, corrosion of nickel can occur simultaneously. This process is known as metal dusting; CO induces the disintegration of Ni due to the diffusion of graphite into Ni and the subsequent burst of the Ni bulk [4,23,26,27]. CO induced carbon deposits occur due to Reactions 3e4 [11,23]. In the presence of steam the water-gas-shift reaction (Reaction 5) also occurs, which further influences the equilibrium [11]. 







#### 2.1. Cell geometry 

Commercial large planar anode-supported SOFC membrane electrode assemblies (MEA) with total areas of 100 mm � 100 mm manufactured by CeramTec, were used to perform the experiments in this study. The anode consisted of a porous, 200e300 mm thick nickel/yttria-stabilized zirconia (Ni=YSZ) substrate and a 10 mm nickel/zirconia (Ni=ZrO2) functional layer. The electrode was a 10 mm thick YSZ electrolyte layer. The cathode was fabricated as a 30 mm lanthanum strontium cobalt ferrite (LSCF) layer. To avoid interaction between LSCF and YSZ, a 5 mm doped ceria (CeO2) barrier layer was added between the electrolyte and the cathode. The cathode area, and thus the active area of the single cell was 90 mm � 90 mm. A cross-sectional view of the cells used is shown in Ref. [5]. 

#### 2.2. Cell housing, test rig and measurement equipment 

The large planar anode-supported SOFC-MEAs were installed in a in-house developed ceramic cell housing. An exploded view of the housing and its components can be seen in Fig. 1a. The main components were the bottom flow field for anode gas distribution, the ceramic frames, and the top flow field for air distribution. Glass powder was used as a seal to separate the anodic and cathodic chambers, while nickel and platinum meshes were used to contact the electrodes. In order to build up the necessary contact pressure, a pneumatic system was installed.

#### 3.2. Carbon deposition experiments 

The experiments carried out with CO=CO2=N2 mixtures (see Table 1) did not result in degradation during the testing period at 750<sup>�</sup> C and 800<sup>�</sup> C. Likewise, in their study, Lebreton et al. [11] did not observe any cell destructing mechanisms during their experiments with wet CO=CO2 gas mixtures. Alazate-Restrepo et al. [23] reported stable operation while fuelling a button cell with Ni=YSZ anode with 100% CO at 800<sup>�</sup> C. Whereas gas mixtures containing CO and H2 did lead to performance degradation and carbon deposition. We observed the same tendency during our tests with fuels containing hydrogen and carbon monoxide. Fig. 4 shows the impact of the gas mixtures CH on the performance and impedance of the tested MEA. During the tests, the amount of CO2 was varied from 

0 vol% to 5 vol%. During these variations, various polarization curves and impedance spectra were recorded, which are difficult to compare as no steady state was identified, and degradation occurred continuously. The reason for the continuous degradation was carbon deposition due to Boudouard equilibrium (Reaction 3) and CO reduction (Reaction 4) [23]. As the CO disproportion favors to proceed at lower temperatures, it is assumed that CO reduction is the major relevant reaction regarding carbon formations in this case [7,21,23]. Alazate-Restrepo et al. [23] claimed the formation of carbon deposits is independent of via polarization generated steam, because CO=H2 mixtures resulted in degradation at OCV and polarization conditions. We observed the same effect during our experiments, as the steam generated by the electrochemical oxidation of hydrogen did not prevent further degradation. However, if enough steam is added to the gas supply a cell protecting effect can be expected, as our previous study with CO=H2O=N2 gas mixtures revealed no significant cell degradation [32]. In these experiments, H2 was produced as a result of the water-gas-shift reaction (Reaction 5) on the anode [32]. To provide exploitable comparative values, the performance and impedance data obtained from the gas mixture CH5 are presented. In Fig. 4a, the upper trends represent the initial (black) and degraded (magenta) performance curves of the gases CH5 and Ref ; the trends below show the differences between the degraded and initial j-V curves. The MEA showed good performance as 369 mA cm<sup>�2</sup> were initially achieved with the gas mixture CH5 at 0.7 V. After 6 h of changing operating conditions, the maximum current density decreased to 269 mA cm<sup>�2</sup> , which is equal to a performance output reduction of 27%. The 6 h of operation with the gas mixtures CH also influenced the cell's performance with the reference gas too, as the maximum current density at 0.7 V decreased from 528 mA cm<sup>�2</sup> to 431 mA cm<sup>�2</sup> or by 18%. The performance degradation with the reference gas is thus much smaller than the performance degradation measured with the gas mixture CH5. Taking a look closer at the degradation voltage trends,

#### 3.3. Scanning electron microscopy 

Scanning electron microscopy investigations were performed in order to examine the impact of the aforementioned experiments on the microstructure of the cells. To identify the chemical elements that could be found on the anode's surface EDX was used. Fig. 6 depicts the backscattered electron images of the SEM investigations of the anode and the Ni contact mesh.
