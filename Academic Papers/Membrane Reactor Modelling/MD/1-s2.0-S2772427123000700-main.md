### **Introduction** 

Energy sustainability is one of the pressing global issues today. Accompanying industrial growth, the energy demand of the world is also increasing. A potential energy resource, such as H2, can be explored to address this problem. H2 is an important energy carrier or fuel for prospective transport and power systems [1,2]. It can produce electricity in a fuel cell [3] without creating pollutants and greenhouse gases [4]. For this reason, hydrogen utilization, in association with a fuel cell, can also achieve decarbonization [5]. H2 can be produced from biomass, hydrocarbons, or water [6]. Fuel processing is needed to extract H2 from these compounds [7]. 

One of the most important and economical ways of H2 production is through water gas shift reaction (WGSR) [8]. It is the reaction of carbon

It is a reversible and exothermic reaction. Thermodynamically, the gas concentrations in the WGSR at equilibrium depend on the reaction temperature and the feed gas concentrations. The WGSR can be categorized into low-temperature and high-temperature reactions [10,11]. Low-temperature WGSR is operated between 200 and 260<sup>◦</sup> C using copper, zinc oxide, and alumina as catalysts [12,13]. High-temperature WGSR uses iron and chromium oxides as catalysts between the temperature range of 310<sup>◦</sup> C and 500<sup>◦</sup> C [14,15]. The WGSR is an exothermic reaction. Operating at high temperatures is thermodynamically favorable and enables rapid reduction of the CO concentration. This study uses a traditional chromium-based hematite catalyst for high-temperature WGSR to achieve rates for industrial applications and resistance against several poisons [16,17]. 

In H2 production technologies, fixed-bed reactors have been researched extensively. The performance of reactors can be predicted and improved by simulation. Using computational methods can markedly reduce research costs and duration in complex studies [18]. Gosiewski et al. [19] studied a membrane reactor through mathematical simulation. They addressed that the membrane reactor technology was promising for H2 production from coal-derived gas under the appropriate temperature. Murgia et al. [20] used a numerical model to compare different catalysts and conditions for WGSR. Their results indicated that the 2-D model was an excellent way to predict WGSR with the commercial catalyst. Palma et al. [21] investigated the influence of carrier thermal conductivity for structured catalysts employing a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) approach. The results showed an intensification of the WGS process, made possible using a structured catalyst with high thermal conductivity. 

Reactor design with fluid flow plays a vital role in practical fluidrelated reactors, such as membrane reactors and wastewater processors. Adrover et al. [22] used the reactor simulation to compare the different directions of sweep flow in a multitubular membrane reactor. Their results suggested that the inverse (i.e., counter-current) direction was better for H2 recovery. Alkhamis et al. [23] studied the spacers for gas-gas separation and proposed that the spacers were an essential component of the membrane systems. Jani et al. [24] investigated the structure of helical flow patterns to separate gas in micromixers. They showed that the helical micromixers were favorable for gas-liquid 

contacting. Chen et al. [25] used evolutionary computation to optimize gas separation. Their results showed that the method could improve the reactor systems efficientl Through the design of the reactor structure and controlled parameters, the performance of reactors can be improved and predicted. This study investigates the crossflow designs of a catalytic membrane reactor (CMR) system. This design is used to deal with more feed gas and tries to increase the CO conversion in WGSR. 

To simulate the performances of H2 permeation and the reaction equilibrium, WGSR is the only reaction in this study. It is known that a membrane can achieve H2 separation and enhance CO conversion [26]. Through the permeation of H2, the CMR can overcome the thermodynamic equilibrium limitation to produce more H2 [27]. For many studies, membranes have been used to separate and recover H2, as shown in Table 1. However, the sizes of the membrane reactor are limited to the capacity of the reaction gases. The crossflow reactor can contain numerous feed gas and multiple catalyst beds to react. The crossflow design is simulated to analyze the advantages and disadvantages of H2 production. The influence of Pd membranes is vital for CMR because it helps investigate the enhancement caused by the membrane for WGSR. The CO conversion is compared to one without a membrane to measure the performance of the membrane. For WGSR, the Reynolds number and steam-to-CO molar ratio (S/C ratio) [28] are important factors that influence the reaction. Better performance can also be predicted by changing operating conditions. The optimized configuration of the multiple-tube palladium membrane was investigated previously in a study by Chen et al. [25]. This study applies a multiple-tube palladium membrane optimized configuration to investigate whether it improves CMRs by considering different operating parameters, including catalytic layer thickness ratio (R/R0), Reynolds number, and steam-to-CO ratio (S/C). The obtained results are conducive to the reactor design and operation to maximize the reactor’s performance.

### **Mathematical formulation** 

_Geometry model_ 

The membrane system used a crossflow structure designed with a single reactor and multi-reactors, shown in Fig. 1 **a** and 1 **b** . This system included one or more concentric tubes, and the concentric tube consisted of an internal tube and a shell. A Pd-based membrane was the internal tube. The catalyst pellets were placed in the shell, as shown in Fig. 1 **c** . The system’s length, width, and height were 16 cm, 10.6 cm, and 1 m, respectively. The diameter of the membrane was 9 mm. In this study, the thickness of the catalytic layer was an important parameter to measure the quantity of catalyst. The thickness of the catalytic layer is represented by _R/R_ 0, as shown in Fig. 1 **d** . _R_ is the whole tube radius (tube+membrane), and _R0_ is the tube radius. 

This work adopted several assumptions to simplify the physical problems: 

- (1) The flow field was laminar in the reactor. 

- (2) The feed gas was homogeneously mixed. 

- (3) The H2 permselectivity of the Pd membrane was infinite. 

- (4) The catalysts were spherical pellets (diameter = Dcat) and the pellets were treated as a homogeneous porous medium with porosity _θ_ and permeability _Kbr_ [29].

### _Numerical method and grid system_ 

The computational model was built with the commercial software COMSOL Multiphysics 5.4. Fig. 2 **a** is the grid distribution of the system for a single tube. Three different mesh numbers of 4, 096 (A), 6, 072 (B), and 10, 563 (C) were examined for the single tube system to test the requirement of grid independence. Due to the significant phenomena of mass transfer, heat transfer, and fluid mechanics around the catalytic membrane tubes and fluid zone, the catalytic layers were built with orthogonal grids. Fig. 2 **b** shows a difference in the pressure coefficient ( _Cp_ ) around the tube between the grid systems A and B. In contrast, the distributions almost overlap between the grid systems B and C. Fig. 3 **a** shows the grid distribution of the four-tube system. Meanwhile, the _Cp_ distributions along the surfaces of the first and fourth tubes under the three different grid systems are compared in Fig. 3 **b** and Fig. 3 **c** , respectively. The mesh structure was the same as the single tube (Fig. 2 **a** ). The results also reveal that grid system B is suitable for numerical simulation and is thus adopted. The developed numerical method is verified using grid system B and shown in Fig. 4. The profiles demonstrate that the simulation results match a previous study’s data [33] very well at S/C = 2 and _Re_ = 100, and the error for each concentration is below 5% between the simulations and the experiments. Accordingly, it validates the numerical method.

### **Conclusions** 

This study used computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to simulate the water gas shift reaction at high temperatures in a catalytic membrane reactor with a Fe-Cr catalyst. The effect of adding a Pd membrane was investigated, as was the catalytic layer thickness ratio, the Reynolds

number, and the steam-to-CO ratio. These parameter adjustments were evaluated by looking at the CO conversion and hydrogen recovery. The important 

- Adding a Pd membrane can efficiently improve CO conversion in the WGSR reactor system. The performance of a system with a Pd membrane increases to 22.9% when the thickness ratio of the catalytic layer (R/R0) is 1.75. 

- The impacts of Reynolds number and S/C ratio on a single tube configuration’s reaction and H2 recovery (HR) are studied. The CO conversion and HR decline when the Reynolds number increases for the CMR because of insufficient contact time with the catalyst during the WGSR. A larger catalytic layer has better CO conversion efficiency, up to 11.4%, but an increase of H2 from the conversion causes H2 dilution due to the limited permeation of the membrane. The profiles of the S/C ratio suggest that elevated steam (higher S/C ratio) causes HR to drop while the CO conversion increases. 

- A tandem of one to four tubes is compared in multiple trials. The linear results show that increasing the number of membrane tubes improves the system’s performance. The Reynolds number influences CO conversion and hydrogen recovery more than the catalyst layer thickness in the four-membrane tube system. A lower Reynolds number provides more contact time for WGSR, leading to slight differences in CO conversion. 

- The optimized configuration performs better for HR at every thickness ratio than the tandem configuration. This revealed that the optimized configuration improves CO conversion performance regardless of the catalytic layer thickness. Choosing the appropriate catalytic layer thickness and Reynolds number can improve the reactor based on the optimized configuration. 

The results all indicate that optimizing operating parameters for a catalytic membrane reactor is vital for efficiently using the water gas shift reaction.
