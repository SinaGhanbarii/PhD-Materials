Developing novel solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) with high stability running at low temperatures is an important objective in SOFC science. In the current paper, a comprehensive physics-based microstructure modeling using scanning electron microscope (SEM) image analysis was performed on several anode-support SOFCs operating at low temperatures with high stability. To bridge the gap in the literature regarding an accurate and realistic modeling, a new model was developed based on the variable fuel and air utilization factors and updated microstructure values (e.g., tortuosity, porosity, pore size, grain size). The model accuracy was verified by a thorough point-to-point validation for eight different cells with the configuration of Ni-YSZ (anode), YSZ (electrolyte), and GDC/PNO (cathode). Different temperatures, hydrogen, and air mass flow rates were used, for which an average error of less than 3% in the I-V curves was achieved. The microstructure of the cells, including cathode thickness (15e26 mm), anode-support thickness (350e460 mm), porosity (39 and 43%), grain size (1.1e1.4 mm), and pore radius (0.9e1.1 mm) were varied. Moreover, the effects of the critical operational and design parameters on the overpotential losses and cell performance were studied. The results show that a hydrogen flow rate of 43 sccm was ideal when the cell operated at 0.9 A/cm<sup>2</sup> and 700<sup>�</sup> C. Moreover, an average anodesupport pore radius of 1.75 mm resulted in the best cell performance. It was also concluded that the electrolyte thickness has a higher effect on the cell performance compared to the cathode thickness.

### 1. Introduction 

Electricity consumption has increased remarkably with the fast development of industrial sectors and population growth in recent decades [1]. Therefore, the capacities of existing power plants in many countries are not enough to address the increasing energy demands. On the other hand, global warming and Ozone depletion problems have encouraged countries to tackle this power shortage through the development of renewable energy resources [2]. Unlike most types of renewable energies, such as wind and solar, fuel cells offer a constant and reliable power source for the grid, attracting more attention [3]. Among various classifications of fuel cells, solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) have the advantages of high energy conversion efficiency, fuel flexibility, high power density, reduced noise, low environmental effect, and high-quality exhaust heat [4,5]. 

There are various advantages of working at higher temperatures (700e900 �C), such as fuel flexibility and highquality heat for the SOFCs [6]. However, such an operation involves two major challenges: a) higher investment cost because of the need for specific materials that are capable of working at high temperatures, b) lower long-term stability 

because of the acceleration of the material diffusion by the temperature increase [7]. Therefore, developing new fuel cells that are capable of working with high efficiency at low operating temperatures (600e650<sup>�</sup> C) is of great importance [8]. This can also promote the application of SOFCs in different sectors, including portable applications. In addition, according to the literature, the microstructure design of the SOFCs is a crucial factor that affects both performance and stability aspects [8]. Size and ratio of grains, porosity, pore size, tortuosity, thicknesses of electrodes, and electrolyte are some of these microstructural features that should be optimized during cell design and fabrication [7].

[9] focused on the optimization of the cathode microstructure to decrease its polarization by using specific mixed ionic-electronic conductors and improve the cell performance by increasing the length of triple phase boundaries (TPBs). ### 2. Experimentation and test setup 

In this section, the experimental works including cell fabrication, experimentation, and SEM images are presented. 

#### 2.1. Cell fabrication 

In this study, two sets of cells (A and B) consisting of four cells in each set were fabricated and tested to systematically compare the effects of the cathode thickness, electrolyte thickness, and anode support porosity on the performance of the cells. The anode supports were made of Ni-YSZ (8 mol% Y2O3) using the slip-casting method. Anode-supports contained different porosities formed by the addition of graphite and pore-former. Following slip casting, thin layers of Ni-YSZ anode functional layer (AFL) and YSZ electrolyte were coated on the supports. For the cathode, GDC was dip-coated on the electrolyte, and subsequently, PNO was infiltrated into the GDC layer. 

As listed in Table 1, the combination of two different anode support porosities (with the addition of 40% and 50% pore former), electrolyte thicknesses (one or two YSZ layers), and cathode thicknesses (one or two GDC layers) led to the fabrication of eight different cells with the specific properties for each cell. The anode support porosity for the set of “cells A” was similar, fabricated with the addition of 40% graphite pore former. Similarly, the set of “cells B” had the same anode support porosities with the addition of 50% pore former. In the series of cell A, the first two cells (A1 and A2) had identical electrolyte thicknesses (one YSZ layer), while cells A3 and A4 had two YSZ layers. The differences between cells A1 and A2 and between cells A3 and A4 were in their cathode thicknesses. The cathode thicknesses of A1 and A3 with one GDC layer were thinner than the ones of A2 and A4 with two GDC layers. Similarly, the cells B1 to B4 were different from each other by the addition of 50% pore former. 

The entire process of the tubular cell fabrication is briefly shown in Fig. 2. As shown, the process started with the fabrication of the anode support, followed by coating the AFL, electrolyte, and a porous GDC scaffold which was infiltrated by PNO. The details of each step are explained thoroughly in the next subsections.
