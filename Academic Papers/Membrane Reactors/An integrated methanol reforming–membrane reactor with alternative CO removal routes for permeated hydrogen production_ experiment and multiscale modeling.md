However, the side reaction of methanol decomposition during MSR generates trace amounts of CO [9], which can directly poison the electrodes of PEMFC, leading to irreversible degradation of fuel cell performance [10]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop integrated systems that combine MSR, advanced CO removal processes, and efficient hydrogen purification technologies to simultaneously achieve deep CO removal, high hydrogen permeation, and system compactness. 

CO removal methods can be generally classified into chemical and physical approaches based on the removal mechanism [11]. Chemical methods achieve CO elimination by converting it into other compounds, such as in CO preferential oxidation (PROX), CO selective methanation (CSM), and water gas shift (WGS). However, these chemical transformations may introduce additional substances into the system. The PROX reaction requires the introduction of additional oxygen into the reaction system [12]. However, due to the competitive oxidation between CO and H2, it is essential to minimize H2 consumption during CO removal to maintain the overall efficiency of the fuel cell system [13]. Xiao et al. [14] developed a methanol processing system consisting of a methanol reformer and a two-stage PROX reactor, and investigated the CO purification performance of the two-stage reactor. The results demonstrated that the system could reduce the CO concentration from 1.6% to 10 ppm. Nevertheless, careful control of the O2/CO ratio is required to avoid promoting the reverse water-gas shift reaction and unnecessary hydrogen loss. The selective methanation of CO is effective for CO removal and allows for system miniaturization. However, it operates under stringent reaction conditions and consumes hydrogen during the process [15,16]. Wang et al. [17] developed a novel multilayer-stacked methanol processing system integrating MSR, catalytic combustion, and selective CO methanation to produce hydrogen-rich gas with low CO concentration, which can be directly supplied to fuel cells. The WGS reaction is typically employed downstream of reforming or gasification processes, as the product gas from these reactions generally contains CO [18]. The WGS reaction not only converts CO but also produces additional hydrogen, thereby contributing to increased hydrogen yield [19,20]. Poggio-Fraccari et al. [21] employed a Cu:Ni catalyst supported on silica for the WGS reaction, and the catalyst sample with a Cu:Ni ratio of 1:2 exhibited excellent CO conversion performance in an 8% CO hydrogen-rich gas. Chen et al. [22] integrated ethanol steam reforming with the WGS reaction in a cross-flow reactor to reduce CO emissions in the reformate gas and enhance hydrogen production. The results indicated that, compared to standalone ethanol steam reforming, the combined system increased hydrogen yield by 1.05 times and improved CO conversion rate by 27.38%. Despite their effectiveness, chemical purification methods typically target CO alone and often neglect other non-hydrogen species. Inert gases such as CO2 and N2, when introduced into the fuel cell, can reduce the hydrogen concentration at the membrane electrode surface and increase hydrogen transport resistance, ultimately leading to a 

decline in fuel cell performance. 

Physical methods, such as pressure swing adsorption (PSA), cryogenic separation, and membrane separation, rely on the differences in boiling points or molecular properties among the components in the gas mixture to selectively separate hydrogen, thereby achieving high hydrogen purity [23–25]. PSA technology is limited by the complexity of the adsorbent regeneration process. Moreover, the large volume and slow response time of PSA units hinder system miniaturization, making them more suitable for large-scale industrial applications rather than portable hydrogen production systems [24]. Cryogenic separation takes advantage of the enhanced differences in boiling points between gas components at low temperatures, enabling more effective separation of target gases [26]. However, this method requires cooling the gas mixture to extremely low temperatures, which involves high energy consumption during the cooling process, results in elevated operational costs, and presents challenges in process control. In contrast, membrane 

separation has emerged as a compact, energy-efficient, and easily integrated technology for hydrogen purification. Membrane separation processes rely on the selective permeability of specific membrane materials to achieve hydrogen separation. Palladium (Pd) membranes and Pd-based composite membranes are particularly attractive for hydrogen purification due to their excellent hydrogen permeability and inherent selectivity [27,28]. Han et al. [29] theoretically investigated the hydrogen separation performance of Pd-Cu and Pd-Ni alloy membranes using density functional theory and molecular dynamics simulations. The results indicated that at various temperatures, both Pd-Cu and Pd-Ni membranes exhibited superior selectivity for H2 over other gases such as N2, CO, CO2, CH4, and H2S. Shi et al. [30] proposed a hydrogen production system integrating MSR with a membrane separator. To avoid the interference of the MSR reaction on membrane separation, a synthetic gas mixture consisting of 73% H2, 25% CO2, and 2% CO was used as the feed gas instead of actual reformate. Experimental results showed that the membrane separator could achieve a hydrogen purity of 99.99%. The optimal hydrogen production conditions were obtained at a methanol feed rate of 1.00 g/min and a system pressure of 5.0 bar. Hou et al. [31] proposed a power generation system integrating methanol conversion with a PEMFC unit based on Aspen Plus simulation. In this system, a hydrogen-permeable alloy membrane with fixed permeability was used to separate and purify the syngas, thereby supplying fuel to the PEMFC. Previous studies have investigated methanol steam reforming in Pd-based membrane reactors, demonstrating the potential of coupling reaction and separation for efficient hydrogen production [28,32,33 However, most of these works focused primarily on reactor configuration and steady-state membrane performance, without examining the combined influence of CO removal reactions and membrane separation on hydrogen yield and CO suppression. In this study, a three-stage integrated reactor combining methanol reforming, CO removal, and Pd-based membrane separation is proposed and experimentally validated to assess the synergistic effects of these coupled processes. 

With the advancement of research on membrane permeation processes, it has been found that CO inhibits the permeation of H2 [33–35 Miguel et al. [36] investigated the effect of CO on hydrogen permeation through a 50 μm thick Pd-Ag (25 wt%) membrane. The experimental results indicated that at a CO concentration of 5%, the hydrogen permeation flux decreased by 57% at 300<sup>◦</sup> C. Moreover, the negative impact of CO on hydrogen permeation was found to be more pronounced at lower temperatures. Tosto et al. [37] conducted tests on the inhibitory effect of CO on hydrogen permeation through 20 μm Pd membrane, 4 μm and 4.5 μm Pd-Ag alloy membranes. The results showed that CO caused a maximum reduction in hydrogen permeation of 32%, 45%, and 40% for the respective membranes. Therefore, coupling CO removal reactions with membrane separation is essential for maintaining high hydrogen flux and system efficiency. 

In response to these challenges, this study proposes a hydrogen production system that integrates MSR, CO removal reactions, and membrane separation technology to achieve efficient and reliable generation of high-purity hydrogen for PEMFC. MSR converts methanol into hydrogen-rich gas, while CO removal reactions convert the CO in the hydrogen-rich stream, ensuring that the membrane separator can efficiently extract high-purity hydrogen. The modular nature of this integrated system facilitates scalability, flexible operation, and potential application in both stationary and portable hydrogen supply scenarios. 

The establishment of an accurate reaction kinetic model is critical for enabling precise system control and facilitating the optimal design of integrated hydrogen production systems. Cu-based catalysts are widely employed in MSR due to their high activity and selectivity, and thus most kinetic studies on MSR focus on Cu-supported systems [9]. Peppley et al. [38] proposed a three-rate kinetic model based on the Langmuir-Hinshelwood (LH) adsorption mechanism for the Cu/ZnO/Al2O3 catalyst, which can accurately predict the generation rates of H2, CO2, and CO under various operating conditions such as different temperatures and steam-to-carbon ratios. # **2. Methods** 

# _2.1. Experimental setup and procedures_ 

# _2.1.1. Experimental system design_ 

The diagram and photograph of the established methanol reformingCO removal-membrane separation hydrogen production system are shown in Fig. 1(a) and (b). The system consists of two independently fed dual-plunger pumps, an evaporator, a preheater, a methanol reforming reactor, a CO removal reactor, a condenser, a dryer, and a membrane separator. 

The methanol reforming reactor and the CO removal reactor have diameters and lengths of 3 × 50 cm and 2 × 50 cm. These reactors were connected in series to form the methanol reforming-CO removal system. By loading different catalysts into the CO removal reactor, the CO conversion performance of the water-gas shift reaction and the CO selective methanation reaction was investigated separately. The thermocouple measures the temperature at the center of the catalyst bed. A double-piston pump is used to deliver methanol and water, which are then directed into the vaporizer with the assistance of nitrogen pressure. The mixed steam is preheated to the required reaction temperature in the preheater and then passes through the MSR reactor and the CO removal reactor, resulting in the production of hydrogen-rich gas with low CO content. The hydrogen-rich gas passes through a condenser and a dryer to remove unreacted methanol and water vapor. Finally, the hydrogen-rich gas enters the membrane separator for deep purification, producing permeated hydrogen suitable for PEMFC. Before the hydrogen production experiment, the catalysts underwent a 12-h hightemperature reduction treatment. The reduction temperatures for the methanol reforming catalyst, water-gas shift catalyst, and CO methanation catalyst were 300<sup>◦</sup> C, 300<sup>◦</sup> C and 500<sup>◦</sup> C. To minimize heat loss, all pipes in the system are insulated with thermal cotton. The composition of the experimental gases was analyzed using a gas chromatograph. 

To ensure the absolute statistical reliability and reproducibility of the experimental data, rigorous measurement protocols were strictly followed. The measurements of all key performance indicators were repeated five times. The experimental results presented in the figures of this study represent the arithmetic mean of these five replicate measurements. Furthermore, to transparently present the precision of the experimental platform, the systematic measurement uncertainties of the primary instruments utilized are comprehensively summarized in Table 1.

# _3.1.4. Hydrogen production performance of integrated system_ 

Fig. 8 illustrates the hydrogen production performance of the system under various experimental conditions. Compared with standalone MSR, both MSR-WGS and MSR-CSM configurations enhance hydrogen permeation yield at the same system pressure. From the comparison between Fig. 8(a) and (b), it is evident that higher _T_ MSR lead to increased CO concentrations in the reformate, which inhibit hydrogen permeation through the Pd-based membrane. In this case, CSM effectively improves permeated hydrogen production. At a system pressure of 500 kPa, the permeated hydrogen flow rate in the MSR-CSM configuration is 14.19% higher than that of MSR alone. A comparison between Fig. 8 (b) and (c) shows that, at the same reforming temperature, a higher S/C not only enhances hydrogen production during methanol reforming but also improves CO removal via the WGS, reducing CO inhibition on membrane separation and increasing overall hydrogen yield. As shown in Fig. 8 (c), at S/C of 1.5 and 500 kPa, the permeated hydrogen flow rate in the MSR-WGS configuration is 12.40% higher than that in MSR. 

It is important to emphasize that the comparison between the MSRWGS-membrane and MSR-CSM-membrane routes is based on their respective practically favorable conditions rather than identical physical parameters. Because WGS is an equilibrium-limited reaction, it requires higher S/C ratios and larger catalyst masses to achieve sufficient conversion. Conversely, CSM is highly active and consumes H2; thus, it requires a strictly limited catalyst mass to prevent excessive H2 loss via CO2 methanation. These results rigorously demonstrate that both CO removal strategies, when operated within their own optimized thermodynamic and kinetic windows, are highly effective in alleviating CO inhibition and improving the overall hydrogen permeation yield of the membrane separation system.

To ensure that the developed kinetic model reflects the intrinsic reaction kinetics without diffusion limitations, experiments were conducted by varying catalyst particle sizes to examine their effect on methanol conversion. Therefore, it can be concluded that when the catalyst particle size is below 30-40 mesh (0.45–0.60 mm), its influence on methanol conversion is negligible.

# **4. Conclusions** 

In this study, an integrated methanol reforming-CO removal–membrane separation hydrogen production reactor was developed and systematically evaluated for PEMFC applications. 

- Experimental results demonstrated that both the WGS and CSM reactions effectively removed CO from methanol reformate gas. When integrated with membrane separation, the hydrogen permeation yield increased by 12.40% and 14.19%, respectively, compared with – 

- the methanol reforming membrane separation system, confirming the strong synergistic effect of chemical CO removal and membrane purification. 

- A dual-rate kinetic model for methanol reforming and a SievertsLangmuir hydrogen permeation model considering CO inhibition were established and validated. These models accurately predicted CO conversion and hydrogen yield under various operating conditions and were integrated into the system simulation, providing a quantitative foundation for system optimization and scale-up design. 

- Sensitivity analysis and optimization identified the influence of key parameters on system performance. The optimal conditions were LHSV of 3.26 h<sup>−1</sup> , S/C of 0.88, _T_ MSR of 224<sup>◦</sup> C, _T_ CSM of 227<sup>◦</sup> C, _P_ of 6 atm, and _T_ M of 450<sup>◦</sup> C, under which the permeated hydrogen molar flow rate reached 3.1182 × 10<sup>−6</sup> kmol/s. 

The membrane separator utilized in this study is equipped with a dense Pd-Au alloy membrane. This Pd-based membrane technology is theoretically capable of yielding ultra-high-purity hydrogen (≥99.99%) [51]. Nevertheless, in practical membrane modules, potential factors such as microscopic pinholes or minor seal leakages could affect the ultimate gas purity. Therefore, while theoretical expectations and equipment information suggest excellent separation potential, further rigorous quantitative verification using highly sensitive analyzers is required in future work to explicitly confirm the exact trace composition of the permeate gas.
