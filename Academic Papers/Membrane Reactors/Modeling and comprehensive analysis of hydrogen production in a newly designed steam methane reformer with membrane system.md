However, the conventional steam methane reforming process is a very mature technology for producing hydrogen, but still, research is going on to make it more efficient and feasible. Researchers have paid a keen interest in using membranes in conventional steam methane reformer (SMR) setups in the last decade. There are the following advantages of membrane steam reforming over conventional steam reforming (Gallucci et al., 2020; Ghasemzadeh et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2020): 

- 1 High hydrogen yield at low temperature 

- 2 High methane conversion at low temperature 

- 3 Production of ultra-pure hydrogen by using perm-selective Pd or Pdalloy membranes 

- 4 Reduction of CO formation because water gas shift (WGS) reaction is favored at low temperatures. 

The advantages mentioned above are based on Le Chatelier’s principle, which describes the fact of the chemical equilibrium shift. According to this principle, methane conversion and hydrogen production are enhanced by the continuous removal of hydrogen from the product stream. 

The potential use of inorganic membranes for operations at mediumhigh temperatures has drawn attention to their development. Numerous membranes have already been produced, specifically for hydrogen gas separation, using various materials, including dense metallic, dense ceramic, porous carbon, dense polymer, mixed matrix membrane, etc. (Castro-Munoz et al., 2018˜ ; Montaleone et al., 2018; Nayebossadri et al., 2019; Sazali et al., 2020; Suzuki et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2015). When compared to those made of other materials, dense metallic membranes, particularly Palladium-based membranes, offer greater hydrogen permeation and selectivity. The inclusion of alloying elements such as silver, yttrium, and copper improves H2 permeability and mechanical strength while having no effect on selectivity (Gallucci et al., 2020; Li et al., 2020). A pressure gradient is a driving force to pass the hydrogen through a membrane. Low pressure is maintained on the permeate side while applying high pressure on the retentate side. The penetration from high to low pressure is induced by higher hydrogen partial pressure. 

Up till now, many researchers have studied the effect of the membrane on hydrogen separation in the steam reforming process. Sanusi 

et al. (Sanusi and Mokheimer, 2019) executed the CFD analysis of hydrogen production through a membrane-based reformer combustor reactor on a 2-D axisymmetric domain. High hydrogen yield was obtained at high reformer pressure, low gas hourly space velocity (GHSV), and low steam to-methane ratio. The CO emission was reduced up to 99% by raising the reformer operating pressure from 1 to 10 bar. Kim et al. (2018) experimentally conducted the steam methane reforming reactions in a Pd-based membrane reactor. The experimentation was performed at the following operating conditions: temperature, 800 K; GHSV, 1000 h<sup>−1</sup> ; and pressure, 400–1114 kPa. Methane conversion and hydrogen recovery rates were 75.1% and 97.9%, respectively. L. Chibane and Djellouli (2011) theoretically analyzed the reaction performance of steam methane reforming in a Pd-based membrane reactor. An isothermal steady-state model was developed to find the optimum conditions for hydrogen production. The obtained results predicted the enhancement of methane conversion by continuous removal of hydrogen from the product stream. A. Iulianelli et al. (2010) performed steam methane reforming reactions in a Pd-Ag membrane reactor by using nickel-based catalyst at 400–500<sup>◦</sup> C temperature and 1–3 bar pressure. The conversion of methane was achieved 50% with membrane and 6% without membrane at 450<sup>◦</sup> C temperature and 3 bar pressure. Moreover, a mathematical model was also developed, successfully validating the experimental results. 

Kyriakides et al. (2014) presented a 2-D, pseudo-homogeneous, and nonlinear model of a fixed bed nickel-based foam catalytic membrane reactor for the steam methane reforming process. They also studied the behavior of structured catalysts and catalysts supported on foam configurations. Patrascu and Sheintuch (2015) performed the experimentation on a membrane reactor equipped with a 175 cm<sup>2</sup> high flux Pd membrane for methane steam reforming reaction. They obtained 90% methane conversion and 80% hydrogen recovery at 525<sup>◦</sup> C temperature, S/C = 3, 10 bar pressure, and 10 NL/min sweep gas flow rate. For heat and mass balances in the system, they also used a 1-D transient pseudo homogeneous model. They could not predict the reason for the reduction of hydrogen recovery in experimental work because CO concentration was less than 1%, which cannot cause inhibition on the membrane surface. Patel and Sunol (2007) numerically investigated the effects of the membrane, sweep gas flow configuration, fuel concentration, and inlet fuel temperature on a thermally coupled membrane steam reformer. After passing through the gas mixture, hydrogen gas reached the surface of the dense metallic membrane. 

Hydrogen permeation through dense metallic membranes is an important process in various applications, including hydrogen storage, fuel cell technology, and hydrogen separation. The hydrogen permeation process involves several steps, including external diffusion of hydrogen molecule to the membrane surface, dissociation to atomic form, adsorption of a hydrogen atom on the metal surface, diffusion of hydrogen atom through the metal, hydrogen desorption, and recombination of hydrogen atoms to form hydrogen molecules (Conde et al., 2017). 

Sieverts’ law, which describes the relationship between the hydrogen permeation rate and the hydrogen partial pressure difference across the membrane, is widely accepted and used to predict the hydrogen permeation rate across dense metallic membranes. The equation derived from Sieverts’ law is a useful tool for predicting hydrogen permeation rate in various applications (Adhikari and Fernando, 2006; Rahimpour et al., 2017).

### _2.2.3. Computational scheme_ 

Due to the use of a high steam-to-carbon ratio (STCR) condition (3.25) that prohibits carbon production and precludes carbon deposition, the effects of carbon deposition were not taken into account in the current calculations (Franchi et al., 2020; de Wilde and Froment, 2012). In this work, we used the finite volume technique available in the commercial CFD software ANSYS® Fluent to discretize the governing equations. Reforming reactions and hydrogen transport across the membrane were implemented using user-defined functions (UDF) written in the C programming language. During the CFD analysis, a pressure-based solver was implemented, and a second-order upwind interpolation method was utilized in order to solve the model equations. For the momentum, continuity, and species equations, the convergence criteria were set to 10<sup>−6</sup> , and the energy equation’s convergence criteria were set to 10<sup>−8</sup> . 

### _2.3. Mesh sensitivity analysis_ 

The hexahedral meshes were generated on the whole computational domain, as presented in Fig. 3. A mesh sensitivity analysis was performed using different element sizes, and the CH4 mole fractions were compared along the length of the reforming tube. The refined mesh was used near the complex structures, burner, and reforming tube to capture the actual phenomena. 

Three different grid resolutions were used to determine the optimal grid size for 3D geometry to get accurate and efficient results with minimum computational cost. The approximate number of cells with 10 mm, 8.0 mm, and 6.0 mm element sizes were 0.40, 0.47, and 1.04 million, respectively. The distribution of CH4 mole fractions presented in Fig. 4 revealed that the mesh with 0.47 million cells was suitable according to computational and time costs. Therefore, the mesh with 0.47 million cells was selected for future simulations.

In this study, the performance of a membrane SMR was illustrated by a detailed 3D CFD analysis. The reforming reactions in the retentate side were incorporated by Xu and Forment’s kinetic model, and Sievert’s law was used to incorporate the hydrogen transport through the metallic membrane. The membrane SMR model was validated successfully with experimental results before detailed analysis. The developed model was then used to study the effects of various operating parameters. Finally, a theoretical cost analysis of hydrogen purification processes; PSA and Pdbased membrane was performed. A summary of the results is presented below: 

- 1 The results predicted that as GHSV grew, methane conversion and hydrogen recovery reduced while hydrogen generation increased. The trade-off between methane conversion and the amount of hydrogen production is required to select the optimum value of GHSV. 

- 2 The process gas inlet temperature also affects methane conversion, hydrogen recovery, and hydrogen production rate. With increasing process gas inlet temperature from 400 to 550 K, the methane conversion, hydrogen recovery and amount of hydrogen production increased from 76.1 to 98.6 (%), 88.8 to 99.1 (%) and 86.6 to 96.7 (Nm<sup>3</sup> /h) respectively. 

- 3 The results also predicted an increase in methane conversion, hydrogen recovery, and hydrogen production rate with an increase in operating pressure. 

- 4 A simulation with a counter-current sweep gas flow configuration gave better results as compared to a co-current operation. 

- 5 The use of a variable hydrogen concentration in the reforming tube with UDF predicted more realistic results. 

- 6 The Pd-based membrane was found to have a lower capital cost than PSA. However, both processes have nearly equal variable and operational costs since the membrane process requires membrane replacement and the PSA technology needs sorbent replenishment and both work at high operating pressure. For a more detailed evaluation of the costs associated with this process, it may be necessary to perform a comprehensive analysis of both the capital expenditure (CAPEX) and operational expenditure (OPEX) involved. However, this study does not encompass such an analysis as it falls beyond its scope. 

The findings of this study provide valuable insights into the factors that affect the performance of the process, which can be useful for optimizing the design and operation of large-scale membrane-based steam methane reforming units.
