## Advances on methane steam reforming to produce hydrogen through membrane reactors technology: A review 

### Adolfo Iulianelli<sup>a</sup> , Simona Liguori<sup>b</sup> , Jennifer Wilcox<sup>b</sup> , and Angelo Basile<sup>a</sup> 

aITM-CNR, University of Calabria, Rende, Italy; bDepartment of Energy Resources Engineering, Stanford University, Stanford, California, USA 

#### ABSTRACT 

Methane steam reforming is the most common industrial process used for almost the 50% of the world’s hydrogen production. Commonly, this reaction is performed in fixed bed reactors and several stages are needed for separating hydrogen with the desired purity. The membrane reactors represent a valid alternative to the fixed bed reactors, by combining the reforming reaction for producing hydrogen and its separation in only one stage. This article deals with the recent progress on methane steam reforming reaction, giving a short overview on catalysts utilization as well as on the fundamentals of membrane reactors, also summarizing the relevant advancements in this field.

### 1. Introduction 

Almost 50% of the world-wide demand for hydrogen (the estimated production of hydrogen is ˜ 55·10<sup>6</sup> tons/year) is derived from natural gas (1). In particular, the current conventional methods for generating hydrogen from natural gas include steam reforming, partial oxidation and autothermal reforming. 

Natural gas steam reforming, called as methane steam reforming (MSR) owing to its main component, represents the most common process for hydrogen generation, despite its negative impact on the climate through emissions of greenhouse gases (GHGs) (2). Today, approximately 50% of the global demand for hydrogen is provided by this process, while nearly 30% derives from oil/naphtha reforming in refinery/chemical industrial off-gases, 18% from coal gasification, 3.9% from water electrolysis, and 0.1% from other sources (3).

Alternatively, hydrogen can be purified by other techniques, namely PSA, cryogenic distillation, methanation, physical scrubbing (for separating initially CO2), membrane technologies, etc., which can ensure the required hydrogen purity. 

As summarized in Table 1, cryogenic distillation does not allow for significant hydrogen separation/purification, although the operating temperature is low (9). The technology based on Pd-membranes makes possible to reach high hydrogen purity and recovery, despite some challenges needs to be overcome such as high membrane cost, low mechanical resistance as well as low tolerance to sulphuric compounds and unsaturated hydrocarbons (10). 

Regarding to polymeric membranes utilization, their limited thermal and mechanical resistance, relatively high sensitivity to swelling and compaction, low tolerance to hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sulfur oxides (SOx) make polymeric membranes-based processes less attractive. 

Currently, the PSA process is the most used conventional technique, allowing for high hydrogen purity. Nevertheless, a loss of hydrogen nearly 20% under PSA operation represents the main drawback of this process. 

Although at both academic and industrial level MSR represents a wellestablished process, many studies are still ongoing. Nowadays, the research on the MSR reaction is mainly devoted to enhance the catalyst performance by producing as much hydrogen as possible, while emphasizing the resistance to catalyst sintering and minimizing the effect of the carbon deposition and sulphur poisoning. Recently, a number of studies is focused on the experimental evaluation of both noble and non-noble metals-based catalysts and the effect of the metal-support interactions (11–14). Among them, a special case is represented by Ni-based catalysts due to their low cost besides other positive characteristics (15–19). 

Furthermore, in the last two decades, numerous researchers studied MSR reaction, investigating the benefits in the use of alternative solutions to the CRs such as of membrane reactors (MRs) (20–33) and, more recently, fluidized-bed membrane reactors (FBMRs) (34–41). Another emerging field of interest is also represented by micro and macro MRs. In particular, the micro-membrane reactors (MMRs), distinguished in planar microchannel, hollow-fiber, and monolithic configurations, represent a very promising area from both a scientific and technological point of view owing to their potential application as on-board fuel reformers for miniaturized PEMFCs or, more generally, for portable electronic devices (42–51). 

Most recently, growing attention has been given to hydrogen generation from various renewable and bio-derived sources, particularly from biogas, in which methane is the main product (52–67). Indeed, biogas is a versatile raw

### 2. A short overview on the catalysts for methane steam reforming reaction 

Although the aim of this work is focused on the recent advances regarding the MSR reaction in MRs, in this section a short overview on the traditional catalysts and the recent developments in this area are summarized. 

In particular, the research done in the last years on MSR reaction demonstrated that most used catalysts show low tolerance to poisons requiring special activation procedures (69). More recently, some studies have been also devoted to the development of new catalysts, that are able to overcome issues related to sintering and coke formation resistance, to allow for hydrogen production near the theoretical limit, to improve stability and reduce the energy input. 

The most investigated catalysts in this field are characterized by single noble and non-noble metal belonging to the VIII group (15,70–81). Currently, it’s not yet possible to give a specific order of catalytic activity and selectivity towards hydrogen production via MSR reaction owing to the disagreement among several scientists involved in this field. Indeed, Jones et al. (82) proposed the following catalytic activity order: Ru > Rh > Ir > Pt, indicating the Pt catalyst as the less active among the other metals. From the opposite side, the study of Wei and Iglesia (83) showed that Pt performance in terms of C-H bond activation are higher than to Ir, Rh, and Ru. 

However, although all these noble catalysts show high catalytic activity and less coke formation, the nickel catalyst represents the most studied and commonly used at industrial scale for MSR reaction owing to its low cost. Indeed, the combination of two metals can be more effective than the single metal-based catalysts in terms of enhanced conversions and hydrogen selectivity, besides resistance to coke formation and higher catalytic activity. As reported by various authors on this topic, in case of bimetallic catalysts utilization, the two metals do not show a deep interaction with each other, but it seems that one of them acts as a dopant. ### 3. Fundamentals of Pd-based membranes 

### 3.1. Membrane and membrane reactor technology in the market 

In recent decades, a wide scientific literature has been addressed towards the study of inorganic membranes, particularly, dense metallic membranes because of their benefits in the gas separation and applications in MRs (10,107,108). In particular, Pd-based membranes have received particular attention owing to their capability to separate hydrogen from other gas, although their large commercialization is still limited because some challenges need to be overcome as the low hydrogen permeability, mechanical resistance, and high costs. 

However, at pilot/industrial scale, one the first commercial applications regarding dense Pd-membranes applied to hydrogen rich-gas purification was realized by Johnson Mattey in 1964, obtaining high grade hydrogen with 0.1 ppm of impurities. Successively, they used this technology for realizing a MR and carrying out methanol steam reforming reaction to generate hydrogen for the Britsh Antarctic Survey.

Composite membranes, based on a dense Pd-layer deposited onto a porous support, constitute a good compromise between dense Pd-based and porous membranes for the hydrogen separation and purification, since they combine the benefits of the two aforementioned inorganic membrane typologies. Indeed, the deposition of dense Pd-layer onto a porous support presents advantages like high H2 perm-selectivity, high permeability, and lower presence of palladium. The supports useful for the Pd-based composite membranes preparation can be based on porous stainless steel (PSS), which are mechanically stable and easy of allocating in a module, or on porous ceramic and Vycor glass supports, which suffer the thermal cycles more than the metallic supports owing to the larger difference of the dilation coefficient between palladium and the ceramic material. 

As a consequence, a PSS-supported Pd-based membrane can offer an optimal compromise among moderate cost, high H2 permeability and perm-selectivity, and good mechanical resistance. Nevertheless, they are characterized by a large pore distribution, which can cause the intermetallic diffusion of palladium, decreasing the permeation characteristic of the membrane. Therefore, the PSS supports are previously conditioned with an intermediate layer useful to decrease their pores size. 

Usually, different techniques can be used for depositing a thin selective Pd-layer and, depending on the technique, there is a threshold on the minimum thickness obtainable by the deposition method. This limit increases with the pore size of the support. 

However, composite Pd-based membranes can be characterized by a series of issues related to inherent instability and caused by the different nature of the materials. One of them is represented by the membrane instability to the interface Pd-layer/support, i.e., the adherence loss, flaking-off, and cracking, potentially caused by the different thermal expansion coefficients and by the selective layer volume variation, due to the hydrogen diffusion in the palladium lattice. Furthermore, other forms of instability are connected to Pdbased membrane micro-structure, generated by the interaction of the selective layer with the support and the gaseous environment. As a consequence, thin membranes are characterized by very small grains and high density of grain borders, which could lead to grain size growing, impurities dissolution, grain borders diffusion, and alloy segregation. 

The main effects of these instabilities are represented by a reduction of the membrane performance and this effect is more evident at high temperature.

In the last decades, several scientific papers dealt with the MSR reaction to generate hydrogen in PBMRs, equipped with palladium or palladium-alloyed membranes, although in recent years most attention has been dedicated to the application of the FBMRs for producing hydrogen. In most recent years, an interesting literature on the recent advances about MSR reaction to produce hydrogen in PBMRs can be found. For example, Saric et al. (27) paid special attention not only to achieve the best experimental results in terms of conversion and hydrogen recovery (98% methane conversion and around 70% hydrogen recovery at 580°C, 28 bar, and 3/1 as steam to carbon feed ratio), but also to investigate the resistance of the composite Pd-based membrane under operation in order to give more details to transfer this technology from bench scale to pilot or industrial scale. In particular, they observed more than 1100 h as life time of the membrane at stable permeation characteristics. 

In the same contest, Dittmar et al. (20) studied the long-life time of a Pdbased composite membrane under operation at different temperatures, demonstrating that the membrane performance are stable at 650°C, meanwhile reaching 70% methane conversion and 65% hydrogen recovery at 16 bar of reaction pressure. 

Iulianelli et al. (67) used a model biogas mixture for MSR reaction performed in a composite Pd/Al2O3 MR to generate renewably and clean hydrogen for fuel cells applications. The composite membrane showed H2/N2 ideal selectivity equal to 4300 at 0.5 bar of transmembrane pressure and T = 400°C. When allocated in the MR module, this solution made possible a methane conversion higher than 30% at 450°C, 3.5 bar H2O:CH4 = 4:1, GHSV = 11000 h<sup>−1</sup> with a quite high hydrogen recovery around 70%. Nevertheless, at the aforementioned operating conditions, the characteristics of H2 perm-selectivity were negatively affected causing an increased presence of pin-holes formation on the Pd-layer of the composite membrane and inducing a H2 permeate purity lower than 70%. Otherwise, at 380°C, 2.0 bar, H2O:CH4 = 3:1, GHSV = 9000 h<sup>−1</sup> although the MR reached a modest conversion (27%), the hydrogen recovered in the permeate side (> 20%) showed a purity around 96%.

### 5. Methane steam reforming kinetic 

In the past, the investigations on MSR reaction have been mainly addressed towards the preparation of catalyst and the process analysis, neglecting both the kinetics and the reaction mechanisms with a consequent lack of information. Therefore, in recent decades, several studies have been realized with the intent to propose coherent reaction mechanism and kinetic data (77,83,130– 135). One of the first accurate studies on MSR kinetics was realized by Temkin et al. (130), who conducted the experimental tests on nickel foil at atmospheric pressure and by varying temperature from 470–900°C. In particular, they discovered that, at the highest temperature, the reforming reaction followed a first-order equation and, at lower temperature, a delay in the rate of hydrogen formation was detected. Ten years later, Xu and Froment (131,132) improved the Temkin’s model developing the most well-known kinetic model for MSR reaction. In particular, Xu and Froment initially provided a path of reactions consisting of 21 rate equations, which were reduced to three by considering the Langmuir-Hinshelwood reaction mechanism. ### 5.1. PBMR and FBMR: Modeling results 

As is well known, the modeling is an important tool for describing, optimizing and controlling complicated system. Currently, several mathematical models for MSR reaction performed in PBMRs and FBMRs are present in the specialized literature (137–159). They are based on different assumptions and reactor configurations and their complexity is a function of the number of conservation equations considered. Generally, the mass and energy balance as well as the continuity equation and momentum balance are required. The first two equations are used for predicting the variation of concentration and temperature; the last two ones for assessing the mass changes due to permeation through the membrane and the pressure variation. 

Each reactor model can be classified according to its complexity. In particular, regarding the PBMRs the model can be categorized according to the number of dimensions, such as one-dimensional (1D), bi-dimensional (2D), or tri-dimensional models (3D). Meanwhile, for the FBMRs, two model classes have been proposed based on pseudo-homogeneous or two-phase approach, respectively (137). 

Generally, 1D model takes into account only the gradient in the axial direction. This model is very simple to solve and it is frequently used (31,138–142). For instance, Simakov and Sheintuch (31) used a pseudohomogeneous 1D model for optimizing, in terms of membrane area and heat losses, an autothermal MR to produce hydrogen via MSR reaction. 

The 2D model is used when it is not possible neglecting the radial gradient, for instance, in the case of mass and heat transfer through the membrane (143–147). Although this model is more accurate, it involves more calculations and, consequently, it is high computing time-consuming. For example, Tiemersma et al. (146) used a CFD 2D model by highlighting the negative influence of the polarization effect on the membrane performance. 

Markatos et al. (148) and Vogiatzis et al. (149) simulated MSR reaction at industrial level for investigating the mass effect and heat dispersion. 

Oyama and Hacarlioglu (144), compared the PBMR performance, simulating with 1D and 2D models, and suggested a criterion for choosing which model should be used at the given operating conditions. An improvement of this work was realized by Marìn et al. (143), who also studied the effect of the operating conditions for optimizing the PBMR performance, highlighting that the space velocity and the reaction temperature can be considered as the most important parameters.

Regarding the FBMRs, the models can be classified according to the following approach (pseudo-homogeneous or two-phase one) (137). 

The first approach proposes conventional multiphase flow models, such as ideal flow, dispersion, and residence time distribution models. The second approach considers two phases, such as bubble and emulsion, suggesting for each phase different governing equations and describing the mass interchange between them. 

The “bubbling bed model” developed by Kunii and Levenspiel (156) and the “bubble assemblage model” proposed by Kato and Wen (157) are the most used two-phase models. In particular, in the first model the bubble phase flows in a plug flow in a well-mixed emulsion phase. In this case, the mass exchange 

between the bubble and emulsion is taken into account by a constant average bubble diameter (39). 

In the bubble assemblage mode, the fluidized bed is subdivided in “n sections,” which are considered as ideally continuous stirred tank reactors.

### 6. Conclusion 

This article pointed out that MSR reaction represents a mature process not only because it was carried out in conventional reactors but also because it involved a strong interest in developing alternative tools and more active catalysts for energy and costs saving. Furthermore, this study has also reported a short overview on the most recent advances about catalysts utilization in MSR reaction to generate hydrogen. In addition, the role of the support has been also discussed in the field of coke formation reduction and catalytic activity enhancement. 

Thus, this article also presented the recent advances about the MR technology, analyzing the different solutions for enhancing the MSR process. In particular, the typical benefits of the MRs have been discussed considering the synergistic combination of two process functionalities in a single unit (the MR), by subdividing the discussion between PBMRs and FBMRs applications. In particular, the functionalities of PBMRs, characterized by very simple configuration have been illustrated as well as the FBMRs, showing enhanced mass and heat transfer rates and favouring more uniform temperature profiles. Nevertheless, for the latter reactor typology, possible bubble-to-emulsion mass transfer limitations can negatively affect the FBMRs performance and the catalyst fluidization can damage the membrane. 

Then, MSR reaction in MRs has been also reviewed to offer a panoramic view on the evolution of the theoretical approach to this process and the recent efforts. 

In conclusion, it can be stated that the progress realized until now on MSR reaction in MRs suggests that other further challenges for MRs commercialization will be met in the future. Nevertheless, from an industrial point of view there are still many issues to be addressed, such as the costs of the membranes and their stability with time on stream.
