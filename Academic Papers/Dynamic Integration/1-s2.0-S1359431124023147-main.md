##### **1. Introduction** 

With the advent of the fourth industrial revolution, drones have begun to be used in numerous fields. In the military sector, because surveillance and reconnaissance drones are small and can be automatically or remotely controlled by computers, they are quickly replacing military aircraft. Drones also play a wide range of roles in the commercial sector, such as in the delivery of goods, agricultural monitoring, atmospheric research, and maritime surveillance [1,2]. There are increasing demands for surveillance and reconnaissance drones not only in the military sector but also in the commercial sector [2]. Investments in advanced drone technologies are also increasing in each country, so the small drone market is predicted to grow annually [2]. The global small drone market was estimated to be $3.68 billion in 2021, and this value is expected to increase by 11.5 % annually until 2030 [2]. As a power source for drones, engines were the most widely used power sources as recently as a few years ago; however, considering the carbon emissions of engines, the number of drones powered by lithium-ion (Li- 

ion) batteries is increasing. However, carbon emissions that occur while mining types of minerals required for use in Li-ion batteries are of concern [3]. Therefore, Li-ion batteries cannot be relied on as completely eco-friendly power sources. In addition, since Li-ion batteries exhibit a low energy density per weight, they are difficult to fly for extended periods [1,4]. In contrast to engines and Li-ion batteries, hydrogen fuel cells are more suitable for drones because hydrogen has a much higher gravimetric energy density than Li-ion batteries, diesel, or jet fuel [5]. However, hydrogen fuel cells exhibit the disadvantage of a slow response, which prevents them from satisfying severe load changes. To compensate for the low dynamic response of fuel cells, a hydrogen fuel cell system should be integrated with Li-ion batteries to fully satisfy the severe load changes of drones. 

Among the various types of hydrogen fuel cells, proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) are mostly used for air mobility since they have the highest power density. A PEMFC exhibits an operating temperature range of 60 to 80<sup>◦</sup> _C_ and generates heat during operation, so the inclusion of a cooling system is needed. Since air cooling systems use only fans to cool fuel cells, unlike liquid cooling systems, which use many types of balance of plants (BOPs), most small drones use air cooling systems to minimize the fuselage weight of drones. However, there is a limitation in expanding the area of the fan, and large-scale fuel cell systems need to use liquid-cooling systems due to the high convective heat transfer coefficient of liquid compared to that of gas. 

Currently, there are a few types of drones powered by hydrogen fuel cell-battery hybrid systems, most of which are small drones [8–11]. However, there are few cases in which large drones have been tested via flight tests. In 2021, the world’s first large aircraft four-seat model, DLRHY4, was developed by the German Aerospace Center. This model used liquefied hydrogen fuel, was powered by an 80 kW PEMFC system developed by Cummins, and successfully completed a two-hour flight test [12,13]. In 2023, the UK company ZEROAVIA succeeded in a 23minute cruise flight test with their 19-seat prototype model ‘ZA600′, which uses the 600 kW PEMFC system [14]. Small commercial drone products, which consist of 30 W to 4,000 W air-cooling PEMFC systems, have been developed by Doosan Mobility Innovation (DMI), Intelligent Energy, Spectronik, and H3 Dynamics [8–11]. 

Controlling the power distribution among multiple power systems is one of the existing difficulties in developing the PEMFC-battery hybrid system. In the case of a large-sized urban air vehicle (UAV), several PEMFCs and batteries will be used for supplying energy to the rotors of the UAV, consisting of the optimal energy management strategy (EMS) methods and the EMSs among these power sources are important. Many kinds of EMS methods like the rule-based method, genetic algorithm, particle swarm optimization, and model predictive control methods have previously been studied by many researchers. The genetic algorithm and the particle swarm optimization, which are methods of creating and evaluating numerous candidate solutions, are not appropriate to this study since the developed PEMFC-battery hybrid system 

requires immediate and accurate control depending on the battery SOC state. In addition, these two EMS methods have the disadvantage that it is difficult to interpret the process even if an optimal solution is derived, making it difficult to understand the hybrid system’s EMS algorithm, making it difficult to modify EMS. The model predictive control method calculates the optimal value based on the current value and corrects the value by predicting the next state but, this method is not appropriate for the system which requires a fast response. The state machine control method is suitable for a PEMFC-battery hybrid system since it can respond to changes in operating conditions and power fluctuations immediately. Hyun et al. studied the EMS method for a PEMFC-battery hybrid drone [15]. They constructed an EMS with a rule-based method that controls the power of a PEMFC and a battery according to the SOC value of the battery. The EMS in this study considered only one PEMFC and a battery, and the flight profile was constructed simply just considering the cruise phase. Xiao et al. developed a simple rule-based EMS for the optimization of the PEMFC-lithium battery hybrid system for UAV [16]. They adopted automatic switches to the DC-DC converter connected to the PEMFC and the lithium battery, to charge and discharge the battery following the SOC of the battery. Their developed EMS was compared with the conventional EMS, and the lifespan of the PEMFC has been extended by 2.1 %. Ren et al. designed the fuel celllithium battery hybrid system-powered vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) drone and simulated it by adopting the control of the state machine EMS[17]. They verified their EMS made the fuel cell run stably to guarantee its safety and lifetime. Shen et al. conducted an experimental study about the dual air-cooled PEMFC stacks by changing the load current of the stack and the air flow rate [18]. The EMS developed by their study is not among each fuel cell stacks but only between the PEMFC and the battery. Kim et al. studied power distribution for a 50 kW PEMFC-battery hybrid vehicle to find out the optimal power distribution rate between a PEMFC and a battery in rapid load change situations and normal situations [19]. By comparing hydrogen consumption and battery SOC according to load changes, they find out the

Previous studies of the basic concept are useful for the optimal EMS of the PEMFC-battery hybrid system. However, the models developed in previous studies were based on empirical models or zero-dimensional steady-state models. Because the main function of a battery in a PEMFC-battery system is to compensate for the low dynamic response of a PEMFC, capturing the dynamic behavior of a PEMFC is crucial for developing an EMS between a fuel cell and a battery. 

There are some previous studies about EMS for dual-PEMFC systems. Xie P. et al. presented a two-layer EMS to enhance shipping fuel efficiency [23]. The first layer uses a load profile consisted in advance, and the second layer distributes the real-time load to multiple power sources to make required power and high-efficiency operation. The strategy of the first layer is developed based on the state machine EMS so, the premade load profile is developed to work differently based on the battery SOC value. By their presented strategy, they checked that fuel consumption was saved by 28 % compared to the state-based method. The proposed two-layer EMS in this study is for the real-time experiment and it works based on the premade load profile, but the strategy has not considered finding optimal power distribution between fuel cells. Garcia et al. studied power distribution between two PEMFCs in a PEMFCbattery hybrid vehicle system [24]. They consisted of three different schemes – equal distribution, maximum power distribution, and optimal distribution, and concluded that their developed optimal distribution was superior among the three schemes. Their developed optimal strategy is constructed for maximizing the efficiency of fuel cells. However, this strategy has not considered battery charging during working conditions. Since this strategy has not considered PEMFC working differences according to the battery SOC, this strategy can not be said as the best strategy in the dual fuel cell–battery hybrid system. Fernandez et al. developed a two-layer adaptive state machine-based EMS for a multiPEMFC and battery hybrid system [25]. The EMS is constructed to work PEMFCs between their maximum efficiency and maximum power points to enhance the system lifetime and use the minimum possible 

number of stacks to generate the required power. They compared their developed strategy and the equal power distribution strategy, and they checked that their strategy showed about 2.4 % less hydrogen consumption than the equal power distribution strategy. The load profile in this study consists of random step changes so, the result can’t be said to be appropriate for drones. 

Unlike other previous studies, in this study, the state machine-based EMS is considered to find out the most efficient operation strategy of the PEMFC-battery hybrid system for drones between two PEMFCs and the battery by investigating the energy consumption by varying the energy management strategies. The PEMFC-battery hybrid system model for propelling a drone is developed and simulated under three different energy management strategies. To accurately predict the dynamic performance of a PEMFC-battery hybrid system during a flight scenario, a one-dimensional two-phase dynamic model of a PEMFC stack, a zerodimensional dynamic model of a Li-ion battery, DC-DC converters, and a DC/AC inverter were developed and integrated into the system. The PEMFC-battery hybrid system model was simulated to predict the power required for the propulsion of the drone according to a flight scenario consisting of five phases: takeoff hover, climb, cruise, descent, and landing hover. The variations in the ambient temperature and pressure were determined according to the variations in the flight altitude during the flight scenario, which were applied to the inlet conditions of the stack cathode. The EMS for the PEMFC-battery hybrid system model has been developed based on state machine control in this study for quick response. Three different EMSs are designed and adopted to determine the optimal EMS between the first stack, the second stack, and the battery.

##### _2.2. PEMFC stack_ 

Because the current PEMFC model used in this study is a modified model that is based on our previous model, the PEMFC model is only briefly described [26]. This PEMFC model is discretized into nine control volumes, namely, the anode solid plate, anode gas channel, anode gas diffusion layer (GDL), electrolyte, cathode GDL, cathode gas channel, cathode solid plate, cooling channel, and cooling plate, in the flow perpendicular direction to resolve the dynamic mass and the energy balance, as shown in Fig. 2. ##### _2.3. Lithium battery_ 

In order for the PEMFC to be utilized as a power source to power the drone, the battery or the supercapacitor should be installed to supplement the low dynamic response of the fuel cell. In a comparison of the power density between the supercapacitor and the Li-ion battery, the supercapacitor is superior than the Li-ion battery. This means supercapacitors are well-fitted for the peak or abrupt power change situation. However, the energy density of the supercapacitor is lower than that of the Li-ion battery. This means that more volume is required for drones to store the same amount of electricity. During the flight scenario, low power required flight phases like the cruise phase, the required power for the hybrid system is lower, so during the flight time, Li-ion batteries can be charged by the power of fuel cell systems. The charged power can be used during the flight scenario, so the flight range can be extended. As a result, since the volume of the drone is limited and the drone aims to fly long distances, the lithium battery is more suitable than the supercapacitor for this hybrid system. 

The type of battery considered in this study is a lithium battery because it exhibits a higher energy density and higher efficiency among the various types of commercialized batteries. The battery model in this paper was developed based on the modified Shepherd model [29]. ##### _2.7. Energy management strategy (EMS)_ 

Among the various EMS types, the state machine EMS is implemented in the model. The state machine EMS enables straightforward understanding and execution of specific energy management actions for each state because it provides a clear and structured approach to control each state. The state machine EMS is an excellent option for managing and following the current state of the system. This approach is advantageous for monitoring energy consumption tendencies based on the system’s current conditions. 

In this study, the efficiency of the PEMFC-battery hybrid system was compared by employing three energy management strategies. Fig. 5 shows the power generation variation in the first stack and the second stack to follow up the power load profiles at various energy management strategies. PEMFC systems provide most of the required power, and batteries compensate for the insufficiency of the dynamic response of PEMFCs during severe load changes. The minimum SOC of the battery for flight was set to 60 %, and the rated SOC range was set from 65 % to 90 % [15]. When the SOC of the battery decreases to under 60 %, fuel cell systems produce additional power to charge the battery. In Case 1, both the first and the second PEMFCs generate an equal amount of power to charge the battery together, and in Cases 2 and 3, only the first PEMFC generates power to charge the battery since both power distribution algorithms use the first PEMFC at first until its power reaches to its nominal power. 

The PEMFC systems continue charging the battery until the battery SOC reaches its minimum value in the rated SOC range. However, when the drone needs to generate high power, the PEMFC systems stop charging the battery, and both stacks and the battery increase power to satisfy the increased load power of the drone. During the flight phase, which requires steadily low load power, the batteries are charged by PEMFC systems because the load power is lower than the maximum power of the PEMFC systems.

##### **4. Conclusion** 

For the high-efficiency operation of a PEMFC-battery hybrid system, a numerical model of the PEMFC-battery hybrid system was developed and used to simulate the performance of three different energy management strategies. The total hydrogen consumption and the final SOC value of the battery were compared among the three cases to determine the optimal EMS for the first stack, the second stack, and the battery. To predict the dynamic performance of the PEMFC-battery hybrid system during flight more accurately, a one-dimensional two-phase dynamic model of the PEMFC stack was integrated with a zero-dimensional dynamic model of the battery. The dynamic correlations between the mass transfer, heat transfer, and electrochemical reaction in PEMFCs were captured. Main findings and summaries are as follows: 

1. When the total load current is not distributed to the two fuel cells, a high current is applied to one fuel cell, resulting in elevated overvoltages. In Case 2, the first PEMFC primarily generates power to meet the total power load across the entire operating region, resulting in the highest current being applied to the first PEMFC. This leads to the elevated overvoltages, ultimately causing the lowest efficiency. During severe load changes, one fuel cell must operate with rapid power load changes, leading to heighten dynamic behavior. As a result, the magnitude of the dynamic behavior of the fuel cell in Case 2 is the highest. 

2. Hydrogen consumption in Cases 1, 2, and 3 was 120.12, 127.32, and 123.13 g, respectively. Consequently, Case 1 had the least hydrogen consumption among the three cases. This is because the total load current was primarily applied to the first PEMFC in Cases 2 and 3. The high operating current of the first PEMFC results in elevated 

   - overvoltages, ultimately leading to higher hydrogen consumption in Cases 2 and 3. 

3. The final SOC values of the battery for Case 1, Case 2, and Case 3 were 59.4 %, 59.5 %, and 59.4 %, respectively. There was no significant difference in the final SOC value of the battery across the three cases. 

4. Consequently by considering both hydrogen consumption and battery SOC, Case 1, which consumes less hydrogen and maintains higher efficiency for both the PEMFC and the battery compared to Cases 2 and 3, is regarded as the best EMS for a high-efficiency hybrid system among the three suggested cases. 

5. The model developed in the study is very useful for providing basic insight into establishing the optimal control of a PEMFC-battery hybrid system for drone propulsion. This study is important for future research on configuring the EMS of fuel cell-battery hybrid power systems for urban air mobility.
